Orientation

What an associate PM needs to own

You already have the CFA framework. This course turns it into desk language: DV01 instead of duration, curve trades instead of curve theories, and breakevens instead of point forecasts.

The five questions every fixed income portfolio answers

  1. How much rate risk? Portfolio duration and DV01 relative to the benchmark or liabilities. This is the single biggest driver of returns.
  2. Where on the curve? Key rate exposures, bullets versus barbells, steepeners and flatteners. Two portfolios with identical duration can behave very differently.
  3. How much credit, and which? Spread duration, DTS, sector and quality mix, and whether the spread on offer pays for expected losses.
  4. Which countries and currencies? Offshore duration, and whether the currency is hedged. For an Australian investor the hedge often changes the answer.
  5. What does the trade earn if nothing happens, and what breaks it? Carry, roll-down, breakeven yield moves, catalysts and a stop.

Module 1 · Foundations

Bond maths and risk measures

Every position on a desk is ultimately described by how much money it makes or loses per basis point. Get fluent in the translation between price, yield and dollar risk.

By the end of this module you can

  • Pick the right duration measure for each instrument, and explain why risk systems report effective duration.
  • Turn duration into DV01 and talk about positions in dollars per basis point.
  • Estimate price moves with duration and convexity, and explain negative convexity.

Price and yield

A bond's price is the present value of its cash flows. Yield to maturity is the single discount rate that makes that present value equal the market price. It is a convenient summary, but it assumes you hold to maturity, receive every payment, and reinvest coupons at the same yield. None of these is guaranteed, which is why desks decompose returns into carry, roll and price change rather than quoting YTM as "the return".

P = Σ (C/f) / (1 + y/f)^k + 100 / (1 + y/f)^n k = 1 … n, f = coupons per year

DV01, the unit desks actually speak

DV01 (also PVBP or BPV) is the dollar change in value for a one basis point move. It is additive, so you can sum it across positions, compare it with the benchmark, and size trades against it.

DV01 = Modified duration × Price × Face × 0.0001 Example: $1m face, price 100, ModDur 8.5 → $850 per bp

Convexity

Duration is a straight-line approximation to a curved price–yield relationship. Convexity measures the curvature. Positive convexity means you gain more when yields fall than you lose when they rise by the same amount, which matters most for long bonds and large moves.

%ΔP ≈ −ModDur × Δy + ½ × Convexity × (Δy)²

Callable bonds and mortgage-backed securities have negative convexity: as yields fall the issuer calls or the borrower refinances, capping the price gain. Investors are paid for selling that optionality through a higher yield, and the PM has to decide whether the yield pick-up is worth it.

Worked example: price, duration and convexity by hand

Take a 3-year bond with a 5% annual coupon, priced at a 4% yield to maturity. Its cash flows are 5, 5 and 105.

  1. Discount each cash flow. 5 ÷ 1.04 = 4.8077. 5 ÷ 1.04² = 4.6228. 105 ÷ 1.04³ = 93.3446.
  2. Add them up for the price. 4.8077 + 4.6228 + 93.3446 = 102.775. The bond trades above par because its coupon exceeds the market yield.
  3. Macaulay duration. Weight each cash flow's timing by its share of price: (1 × 4.8077 + 2 × 4.6228 + 3 × 93.3446) ÷ 102.775 = 294.087 ÷ 102.775 = 2.86 years.
  4. Modified duration. 2.86 ÷ 1.04 = 2.75. A 1% rise in yield cuts the price by about 2.75%.
  5. DV01. On $10m face: $10m × 1.02775 × 2.75 × 0.0001 ≈ $2,828 per bp.
  6. Convexity. Σ CF × t(t+1) ÷ (1+y)^(t+2), divided by price: (8.890 + 25.644 + 1035.636) ÷ 102.775 = 10.41.
  7. Check a 100 bp rise. Duration says −2.75%. Convexity adds ½ × 10.41 × 0.01² = +0.05%. Estimate −2.70%. Repricing at 5% gives exactly 100.00, a fall of 2.70%. The two-term estimate is almost perfect.

Set the lab above to a 5% coupon, 4% yield, 3 years and a +100 bp shock to see the same logic on a semi-annual bond.

Yield measures and their traps
  • Current yield = annual coupon ÷ price. Ignores the pull to par. Useful only as a measure of cash income.
  • Yield to maturity is the internal rate of return on the bond if held to maturity with coupons reinvested at the same yield. Realised return differs whenever reinvestment rates differ or you sell early.
  • Yield to call / yield to worst. For callable bonds, compute the yield to each call date and to maturity; the lowest is yield to worst. Premium callables are usually quoted to call.
  • Compounding conventions. ACGBs and Treasuries quote semi-annual yields. To compare with an annual-pay bond, convert: (1 + y/2)² − 1. A 4.30% semi-annual yield is 4.35% annual.
  • Horizon yield. The return actually earned over your holding period, given your reinvestment and exit yield assumptions. This is the number that goes into a CME.

A common interview trap: "a bond yields 6%, so you will earn 6%". Only if you hold to maturity, nothing defaults, and you reinvest every coupon at 6%.

Clean price, dirty price and accrued interest

Bonds are quoted on a clean price, which excludes interest accrued since the last coupon. The buyer pays the dirty (full) price = clean price + accrued interest, and receives the whole next coupon.

Accrued = coupon per period × days since last coupon ÷ days in period

Quoting clean avoids a saw-tooth pattern in prices around coupon dates. Duration and DV01 should be applied to the dirty price, which is what the position is actually worth. ACGBs go ex-interest shortly before each coupon (7 days), after which a buyer does not receive the next coupon and accrued interest turns negative.

Money duration and portfolio duration

Money duration = modified duration × full price × position size. It is DV01 × 10,000, the dollar loss for a 1% (100 bp) move. Portfolio DV01 is the sum of position DV01s. Portfolio duration is approximately the market-value weighted average of position durations, which assumes every bond's yield moves by the same amount.

Common mistakes
  • Using modified duration for callables, MBS or anything with options. Use effective duration.
  • Quoting duration when the question is about dollars. A 0.1-year overweight on $5bn is $500k per bp; on $50m it is $5k. Always translate.
  • Forgetting that DV01 depends on price. A bond at 80 has less DV01 per face than the same duration at 110.
  • Treating a floating rate note as long duration because it has a long maturity. Rate duration is time to reset; spread duration is to maturity.
  • Ignoring convexity for large moves or long bonds. For a 30-year bond and a 200 bp move, the duration-only estimate is badly wrong.
Module 2 · Term structure

Shape, slope and curvature

The curve is where monetary policy, growth, inflation and risk appetite meet. Learn to read it, name its moves and pull forward rates out of it.

By the end of this module you can
  • Move between spot, par and forward rates, and bootstrap a spot curve by hand.
  • Explain why the forward curve is the breakeven for every rates view.
  • Describe any curve using level, slope and curvature, and name a move (bull steepener and so on).
  • Link curve shape to the policy and business cycle, in Australia and the US.

Three curves from the same prices

  • Spot (zero) curve: the yield on a single cash flow at each maturity. It is the correct discount curve.
  • Par curve: the coupon at which a bond of each maturity prices at 100. This is what screens usually quote for benchmark bonds.
  • Forward curve: the rate implied today for borrowing between two future dates. Spot rates are averages of forwards, so when the spot curve slopes up, forwards sit above it.
(1 + S₂)² = (1 + S₁)(1 + ₁f₁) → 1y1y forward 5y5y ≈ (10 × S₁₀ − 5 × S₅) / 5 (continuous compounding)

Spot rates are extracted by bootstrapping: price the shortest bond to get the first spot rate, then use it to strip the next bond's earlier coupons, and so on. Forward rates are breakeven rates. If rates evolve exactly as forwards imply, every maturity earns the same return over the next period. That makes the forward curve the natural benchmark for any view: you make money on duration only if yields end up below the forwards, not below today's spot yields.

In practice the observed curve is expected policy rates plus a term premium, with habitat effects on top. Module 3 separates those pieces.

Level, slope and curvature

Litterman and Scheinkman (1991) showed that three factors explain nearly all the variation in government bond returns. Level (all yields move together) dominates. Slope (short and long ends move by different amounts) comes a distant second. Curvature (the belly moves relative to the wings) explains a small residual. This is why desks think in parallel duration first, then steepeners and flatteners, then butterflies.

Slope: 2s10s = y₁₀ − y₂ 5s30s = y₃₀ − y₅ Curvature: 2s5s10s fly = 2 × y₅ − y₂ − y₁₀ (positive = belly cheap)

Worked example: bootstrapping a spot curve

Par yields (annual coupons): 1-year 3.00%, 2-year 3.50%, 3-year 3.90%.

  1. 1-year spot. A 1-year par bond has one cash flow, so S₁ = 3.00%.
  2. 2-year spot. The 2-year par bond pays 3.5 and 103.5. Discount the first at S₁: 3.5 ÷ 1.03 = 3.3981. The rest of the price must be the second cash flow: 103.5 ÷ (1 + S₂)² = 100 − 3.3981 = 96.6019. So (1 + S₂)² = 1.07141 and S₂ = 3.509%.
  3. 3-year spot. 3.9 ÷ 1.03 + 3.9 ÷ 1.07141 = 7.4265. So 103.9 ÷ (1 + S₃)³ = 92.5735, giving (1 + S₃)³ = 1.12234 and S₃ = 3.922%.
  4. Forwards. 1y1y = 1.07141 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 4.02%. 2y1y = 1.12234 ÷ 1.07141 − 1 = 4.75%.

Pattern to remember: on an upward-sloping curve, forward > spot > par. On an inverted curve the order reverses. Real systems bootstrap from many bonds or swaps and smooth the result with a fitted model such as Nelson–Siegel–Svensson or splines.

Why forwards are the breakeven for every view

Using the example above, compare two one-year strategies: (a) buy a 1-year bill at 3.00%; (b) buy a 2-year zero at 3.509% and sell it after a year, when it is a 1-year zero.

Strategy (b) returns the same as (a) only if the 1-year yield in a year's time is 4.02%, the 1y1y forward. If it is lower, (b) wins; if higher, (a) wins. So:

  • Extending duration pays off only if yields end below the forwards, not below today's yields.
  • On a steep curve, being short duration is expensive: you give up carry and roll every day you wait.
  • On an inverted curve, the forwards are below spot, so a long duration position loses money if yields simply stay where they are.

Whenever someone proposes a trade, ask "what do the forwards say?" before "what do you think will happen?".

The 10-year sector, by contrast, is heavily influenced by US Treasuries and global term premia. That is why the ACGB–UST 10-year spread is a trade in its own right.

Principal components and hedged curve trades

Principal component analysis (PCA) on daily yield changes finds uncorrelated factors. The first (level) loads almost equally on every tenor; the second (slope) loads with opposite signs at the short and long ends; the third (curvature) loads on the belly against the wings.

This matters for trade construction:

  • A DV01-neutral steepener is immune to a parallel move, but not to the way the curve actually moves. Historically, the short end moves more than the long end when yields fall, so even a DV01-neutral steepener has some residual level exposure.
  • A 50/50 butterfly (sell 2 × DV01 of the belly, buy 1 × DV01 in each wing) is neutral to parallel shifts but still exposed to slope.
  • A PCA-weighted fly chooses wing weights so the trade has zero exposure to both the level and slope factors, isolating curvature. It is the cleaner way to express "the 5-year is cheap".
Common mistakes
  • Discounting cash flows with par yields instead of spot rates.
  • Judging a trade against today's yields instead of the forwards.
  • Reading an inverted curve as a timing signal. The lag to recession has ranged widely, and QE-distorted term premia can flatten a curve with no recession signal.
  • Sizing curve trades on notional rather than DV01.
Module 3 · Premia

Term premium

A long yield is compensation for two things: the path of short rates you expect over its life, and the extra return you demand for locking money up at a fixed rate instead of rolling cash.

By the end of this module you can
  • Split a long yield into expected policy rates and a term premium.
  • Explain how term premia are estimated and why the estimates disagree.
  • Name the drivers of term premium and relate them to duration and multi-asset hedging decisions.
yₙ = average expected short rate over n years + term premiumₙ (the "risk-neutral" yield)

The term premium is the expected excess return from holding a long bond instead of rolling short bills. It cannot be observed directly, because expected short rates cannot be observed directly. It has to be estimated.

How it is estimated

  • Affine term structure models. The ACM model (Adrian, Crump and Moench, published by the New York Fed) and Kim–Wright (Federal Reserve Board) are the two most cited. Both fit yields with a small number of factors and back out expected rates.
  • Survey-based. Subtract economists' forecast average short rates from observed yields. Simple and transparent, but surveys are slow and can be biased.
  • Market-implied. Compare long forwards (such as 5y5y) with a judgement of the neutral policy rate. The gap is a rough term premium.

Different models can disagree by 50 bp or more on the level. Treat the estimates as a regime indicator and look at changes and direction, not decimal places. For current values, check the NY Fed's ACM series directly.

Worked example: decomposing a 5-year yield

Suppose the 5-year yield is 4.00%, and the market's expected cash rate path is 3.60%, 3.35%, 3.25%, 3.25% and 3.25% for the next five years.

  1. Average expected short rate = (3.60 + 3.35 + 3.25 + 3.25 + 3.25) ÷ 5 = 3.34%.
  2. Term premium = 4.00% − 3.34% = 66 bp.
  3. If a hawkish surprise lifts every expected rate by 25 bp and the yield rises to 4.25%, the term premium is unchanged. This is a policy repricing.
  4. If the yield rises to 4.25% with no change in expected rates, the term premium has risen to 91 bp. This is a premium repricing, typical of supply or inflation-uncertainty shocks.

The two cases call for different responses. Policy repricing shows up in OIS and cash-rate futures. Premium repricing shows up as a steeper curve and a higher 5y5y forward with little change at the front end.

Why the term premium can be negative

A negative term premium means investors accept a lower expected return on long bonds than on rolling bills. That sounds irrational until you think of bonds as insurance:

  • When stocks and bonds are negatively correlated, long bonds rise in value exactly when the rest of a portfolio falls. Investors will pay for that protection, as they pay a premium for any insurance.
  • Price-insensitive buyers (central banks under QE, reserve managers, pension funds hedging liabilities, banks holding liquid assets) buy duration regardless of expected return.
  • When inflation is stable and credible, the inflation risk premium shrinks toward zero.

All three conditions held for much of 2012 to 2020. The inflation shock of 2021 to 2023 reversed the first and third, and QT reversed the second.

Nominal term premium = real term premium + inflation risk premium

Inflation-linked bonds let you split the premium further. A nominal yield contains the expected real rate path, expected inflation, a real term premium and an inflation risk premium (IRP). A linker yield contains only the real parts.

Breakeven inflation = nominal yield − real yield = expected inflation + IRP − linker liquidity premium

When breakevens rise but surveys of inflation expectations do not, the IRP is usually doing the work. Linkers are less liquid, so part of the breakeven is a liquidity effect that pushes it below true expected inflation, most visibly in stress.

Using the term premium in portfolio decisions
  • How much duration to own. Expected excess return on duration is roughly the term premium. When it is thin, strategic duration is poorly paid; when it is high, owning duration is easier to justify even without a rate-cut view.
  • How reliable bonds are as a hedge. Track the rolling correlation of bond and equity returns. In a positive-correlation regime, a multi-asset portfolio's defensive sleeve needs other diversifiers (cash, gold, trend strategies, currency).
  • Where on the curve. Term premium is concentrated at longer maturities. A steepening driven by premium tends to favour owning the front and belly over the long end.
  • Cross-market. Compare hedged term premia across the US, Australia and Europe to choose where to own duration.

Term premium estimates are model outputs, revised as data arrive. Use their direction and regime, not the second decimal.

Module 4 · Decomposition

Anatomy of the yield curve

A government bond yield is not one number. It is a bundle of separate pieces: where policy rates are expected to go, inflation, the premium for bearing duration risk, convexity, and the supply and demand for that particular bond. Breaking a yield into these pieces is how a PM works out what the curve is saying and which part of it to trade.

  • Read the family of curves (OIS, government, swap, semi, credit) and what the spreads between them mean.
  • Interpret the curve's shape without falling into common misreadings.
  • Attribute a change in yields to its components using OIS, breakevens and inflation expectations.

Breaking down a move

The identity that ties everything together: a change in the nominal yield equals the sum of the changes in its four main components.

Δ nominal yield = Δ expected real path + Δ expected inflation + Δ real term premium + Δ inflation risk premium Observed: Δ breakeven = Δ expected inflation + Δ inflation risk premium Δ real yield = Δ nominal − Δ breakeven Δ term premium = Δ nominal − Δ expected nominal path

So with four observable inputs (the change in the nominal yield, the change in the expected policy path, the change in breakevens, and the change in survey or model inflation expectations) you can fill in every piece.

Worked example: decomposing a 10-year yield

The 10-year government bond yields 4.30%. A term structure model puts the average expected cash rate over the next ten years at 3.40%. The 10-year linker breakeven is 2.55%, and surveys put average expected inflation over ten years at 2.45%.

  1. Term premium = 4.30% − 3.40% = 90 bp.
  2. Inflation risk premium ≈ breakeven − expected inflation = 2.55% − 2.45% = 10 bp (ignoring the linker liquidity premium, which would make the true figure a little higher).
  3. Real term premium = 90 − 10 = 80 bp.
  4. Expected real policy rate = 3.40% − 2.45% = 0.95%.
  5. Check: real yield = 4.30% − 2.55% = 1.75% = 0.95% + 0.80%. Nominal = 0.95 + 2.45 + 0.80 + 0.10 = 4.30%.

So most of the 10-year yield is expected inflation and the real term premium. The expected real policy rate is under 1%. That tells you the yield is high mainly because investors want paying for duration, not because they expect tight policy for a decade. In this model, convexity and technicals are absorbed into the term premium estimate; a fuller model would show them separately.

Worked example: what drove last week's sell-off?

The 10-year rose 20 bp. Over the same week, the model's expected average cash rate rose 5 bp, the 10-year breakeven rose 8 bp, and survey inflation expectations rose 2 bp.

  • Expected nominal path: +5 bp, split into +2 bp expected inflation and +3 bp expected real rate.
  • Term premium: 20 − 5 = +15 bp, split into +6 bp inflation risk premium (8 − 2) and +9 bp real term premium.
  • Real yield: 20 − 8 = +12 bp.

Three-quarters of the move was term premium. This was not a hawkish policy repricing; it looks like a supply, fiscal or global premium shock. That matters for the response: a premium-driven sell-off usually steepens the curve and argues for owning the front end over the long end, rather than a broad duration cut. Convexity bias explained

Because a bond's price is a convex function of its yield, uncertainty about future yields raises its expected price. Investors therefore accept a slightly lower yield on long bonds than the expected path and term premium alone would imply. The effect grows with the square of maturity and with rate volatility.

In a one-factor model with mean reversion a and normal vol σ: convexity bias(T) = σ² ÷ (2a²) × [1 − 2(1 − e^(−aT)) ÷ (aT) + (1 − e^(−2aT)) ÷ (2aT)] σ = 80 bp, a = 0.05: about 7 bp at 10 years, about 36 bp at 30 years

This is one reason very long yields often sit below the 20-year point (the long end "droops"), even when the term premium is positive. It also means part of the long end's apparent richness is fair value, not a trading opportunity.

Technicals: the bond-specific layer
  • Supply: heavy issuance in a sector cheapens it. Watch AOFM tenders and syndications, state budgets, and US refunding announcements.
  • Futures deliverability: bonds in a futures basket, especially the cheapest to deliver, tend to trade rich.
  • Repo specialness: bonds that are special in repo trade rich because holders earn extra by lending them.
  • On-the-run premium: the newest benchmark is the most liquid and trades rich to older bonds.
  • Safe-haven and regulatory demand: bank liquidity rules and flight-to-quality flows depress government yields relative to OIS (a convenience yield).
  • Index events: month-end extensions and index inclusion create predictable buying.

These effects are usually a few basis points, but they decide which bond you use to express a view, and they are the raw material for relative value trading.

A one-minute read of any curve
  1. Front end: what is priced for the next three meetings and the next year? How does it compare with the central bank's own guidance?
  2. Slope: 2s10s and 5s30s. Steepening or flattening, bull or bear?
  3. Forwards: where is 5y5y relative to your estimate of the neutral rate? Above means positive term premium.
  4. Real versus breakeven: is the move in real yields or in inflation compensation?
  5. Spreads: government–OIS, swap spreads and semis. Are technicals or supply at work?
  6. Cross-market: how did US Treasuries move, and how much of the local move followed them?
  7. Conclusion: one sentence on what the curve is pricing and what would make it wrong.
Common misreadings
  • "Long yields are high, so the market expects high rates." Not if the term premium is doing the work. Check the forwards against neutral and the front end.
  • "The curve inverted, so a recession is coming." Possibly, but a depressed term premium can invert the curve on its own, and the timing has been unreliable.
  • "Breakevens rose, so inflation expectations rose." Some of it may be inflation risk premium or a change in linker liquidity.
  • "10-year OIS shows what the central bank will do." Only the short end of the OIS curve is close to pure expectations; long OIS includes term premium.
  • "The bond is cheap to the curve, so buy it." It may be cheap because of supply, deliverability or repo, which can persist.
Doing this with real data
  • Policy expectations: cash-rate futures and short-dated OIS (Bloomberg WIRP for meeting-by-meeting pricing).
  • Term premium: the New York Fed publishes the ACM estimates for US Treasuries. Public estimates for Australia are less available, so many desks build their own from ACGB yields and OIS, or use the US estimate as a proxy for the global component.
  • Breakevens and real yields: TIBs versus nominal ACGBs; TIPS versus Treasuries (FRED has daily US breakevens).
  • Inflation expectations: the RBA's own forecasts, surveys of market economists and consumers, and the Survey of Professional Forecasters in the US.

The reaction function

A central bank's reaction function is how it sets rates in response to inflation, activity and financial conditions. The Taylor rule is the simplest description and a useful anchor:

i = r* + π + 0.5 × (π − π*) + 0.5 × output gap r* = neutral real rate, π = inflation, π* = target

Central banks do not follow any rule mechanically. They look at underlying (trimmed-mean) inflation rather than headline, weigh the labour market heavily, respond to financial stability, and move gradually. The useful question is not "what does the rule say?" but "what would change the bank's mind, and is that priced?"

How policy transmits

  • Short rates and funding costs: the policy rate sets overnight rates, which flow into bank bills, deposits and loans.
  • Expectations: forward guidance moves the expected path, and so the front end of the curve, before any rate changes.
  • Asset prices and the currency: lower rates lift asset prices and weaken the currency, easing financial conditions.
  • Household cash flow: in Australia most mortgages are variable rate, so rate changes hit household budgets within months. In the US, most mortgages are 30-year fixed, so transmission is slower and works through new borrowing and refinancing.

Reading what is priced

Market pricing of the policy path comes from overnight index swaps (OIS) and futures:

  • ASX 30 Day Interbank Cash Rate Futures are priced as 100 minus the average cash rate over the contract month. Because a meeting falls part-way through the month, the implied rate after the meeting has to be backed out (the lab below does it).
  • AUD OIS gives the expected average cash rate over longer horizons.
  • US: fed funds futures and SOFR futures; SOFR options for rate volatility. Bloomberg's WIRP function summarises implied probabilities by meeting.

Balance sheet policy

Quantitative easing (buying government bonds with newly created reserves) lowers long yields mainly by removing duration from private investors (the portfolio balance channel) and by signalling that rates will stay low. Quantitative tightening reverses this, either by letting bonds mature without reinvestment or by selling them. QT adds to the supply the private sector must absorb, which tends to lift term premium, and drains reserves from the banking system, which can tighten money markets.

Fiscal policy and issuance

Deficits must be funded by bond issuance. Watch the AOFM's issuance program in Australia, state budgets (which drive semi supply), and the US Treasury's quarterly refunding announcements, which set the mix of bills and coupon bonds. A shift toward longer maturities adds duration supply and tends to steepen the curve.

Worked example: meeting probability

The cash rate is 3.60%. The RBA meets on the 3rd of a 31-day month, with the decision applying from the 4th. The futures contract for that month trades at 96.43, an implied average rate of 3.57%.

  1. Days at the old rate: 3. Days at the new rate: 28.
  2. Average = (3 × 3.60% + 28 × new rate) ÷ 31 = 3.57%, so the new rate = (31 × 3.57% − 3 × 3.60%) ÷ 28 = 3.567%.
  3. Implied change = −3.3 bp. As a share of a 25 bp cut: 3.3 ÷ 25 ≈ 13% probability of a cut.

When the meeting is late in the month, most of the month is at the old rate and the futures barely move, so small price errors produce large probability swings. Desks then use the next month's contract, or OIS dated to the meeting.

Worked example: Taylor rule

Neutral real rate 1.0%, trimmed-mean inflation 3.0%, target 2.5%, output gap −0.5%.

i = 1.0 + 3.0 + 0.5 × (3.0 − 2.5) + 0.5 × (−0.5) = 4.0%

If the cash rate is 3.6%, policy is looser than this rule suggests. That does not mean hikes are coming: the neutral rate is uncertain by a percentage point or more, and the output gap is estimated with error. Use the rule to frame the debate, and look at how the gap changes as data arrive.

Neutral rate: why it matters for the long end

The neutral rate is the policy rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. It cannot be observed, only estimated (for example with the Laubach–Williams model). Its level anchors where the market thinks rates end up, so it drives forwards such as 5y5y. A market that raises its estimate of neutral lifts the long end even if near-term policy is unchanged. Many estimates rose after 2022 as investors reassessed pre-pandemic assumptions about permanently low rates.

How to trade central bank events
  • Before the meeting: know what is priced (probability and size), what the consensus of economists expects, and what the statement or press conference could change about the path beyond this meeting.
  • The decision usually matters less than the guidance. A cut that is fully priced but delivered with hawkish language can lift yields.
  • Asymmetry: if a move is 90% priced, the payoff from being right is small and from being wrong is large. Options on futures express this better than outright positions.
  • Cross-market: RBA surprises move the ACGB front end and AUD; they rarely move US Treasuries. US surprises move everything.
Inflation dynamics in brief
  • Headline versus core: headline includes volatile food and energy; trimmed mean and core measures show persistent trends.
  • Goods versus services: goods inflation follows supply chains and the currency; services inflation follows wages and is stickier.
  • Expectations: surveys and market breakevens. Central banks worry most when long-term expectations drift.
  • Wage–price dynamics: unit labour costs (wages relative to productivity) drive medium-term services inflation.
  • Administered prices and government rebates (energy rebates in Australia, for example) can distort headline CPI temporarily; central banks look through them.

Repo

A repurchase agreement is a sale of a bond with an agreement to buy it back at a higher price on a later date. Economically it is a secured loan: the cash lender receives the bond as collateral, and the difference between the sale and repurchase prices is the interest (the repo rate).

  • Haircut: the lender advances less than the bond's value (say 98%), to protect against a price fall. Haircuts limit leverage and rise in stress.
  • General collateral (GC): the repo rate for any acceptable government bond. It tracks the overnight rate closely.
  • Special: when a particular bond is in heavy demand (to cover shorts or deliver into futures), lenders of that bond can borrow cash at below the GC rate. Specialness = GC rate − special rate. Holders of special bonds earn extra return by lending them.
  • Reverse repo is the other side: lending cash and receiving the bond. It is how you borrow a bond to short it.
  • The RBA conducts its open market operations largely through repo.

Funded bond position

Why funding markets matter to a PM

  • Carry: the return on any leveraged bond position is coupon income minus the repo rate. Futures prices embed this carry.
  • Leverage: hedge funds, LDI funds and bank desks finance bonds in repo. When haircuts rise or funding dries up, they have to sell, which spreads stress into bond markets.
  • Liquidity management: a portfolio that uses derivatives needs cash or eligible collateral for margin calls.
  • Bank credit: bank bills, NCDs and FRNs are among the largest holdings in Australian cash and short-duration funds, so bank funding conditions directly affect their returns.

Worked example: a repo trade

You buy $10m face of a bond at a dirty price of 101.50 and finance it overnight in repo at 3.55% with a 2% haircut.

  1. Market value: $10.15m. Cash borrowed: 98% × $10.15m = $9.947m. Your own capital: $203,000.
  2. Overnight interest (AUD, Act/365): $9.947m × 3.55% × 1 ÷ 365 = $967.
  3. The bond's coupon accrues at, say, 4.50% on $10m face: $10m × 4.5% ÷ 365 = $1,233 a day.
  4. Net carry ≈ $266 a day on $203,000 of capital, before any price change. But the position's DV01 is still that of $10m of bonds: at a duration near 7, about $7,000 per bp, so a single basis point rise in yield wipes out almost four weeks of carry.
Specialness and the futures delivery squeeze

Suppose GC repo is 3.55% and a bond trades special at 2.80%. Specialness is 75 bp. A holder can lend the bond in repo, borrow cash at 2.80%, and invest it at 3.55%, earning 0.75% a year on the bond's value. The bond's price rises to reflect this (it trades rich to the fitted curve). Specialness often appears in the cheapest-to-deliver bond near futures delivery, in newly issued benchmark bonds, and in bonds with large short positions. Being short a special bond can be very expensive.

Forward price and implied repo

The fair forward price of a bond is its spot price plus financing cost minus coupon income over the period:

Forward ≈ Spot dirty price × (1 + repo × t) − coupons received (with interest)

Working backwards from a futures price gives the implied repo rate: the financing rate at which buying the bond and selling futures breaks even. If implied repo exceeds actual repo, buying the basis (long bond, short futures) earns a positive return. This is the heart of the US Treasury basis trade.

The Treasury basis trade and March 2020

Hedge funds buy cash Treasuries, finance them in repo, and sell futures against them, earning the small gap between implied and actual repo, magnified by high leverage. Asset managers are often the natural buyers of futures on the other side. In March 2020, a dash for cash hit Treasuries, repo margins rose, and basis positions were unwound into an illiquid market, contributing to severe dysfunction until the Fed bought Treasuries in very large size. Regulators have focused on this trade since, including the US move toward central clearing of Treasury repo and cash trades.

Money market funds and runs

In September 2008, the Reserve Primary Fund in the US "broke the buck" (its net asset value fell below $1) because of losses on Lehman Brothers paper, triggering a run on prime money funds and freezing commercial paper markets. Similar outflows hit prime funds in March 2020. US reforms have since pushed investors toward government money funds and added liquidity requirements and fees on prime funds. The lesson for PMs: vehicles that promise stable value and daily liquidity on assets that are not perfectly liquid are vulnerable to runs.

Inflation-linked bonds in detail

A capital-indexed bond (TIBs, TIPS) pays a fixed real coupon on a principal that is uplifted by CPI. Both coupons and the final payment grow with inflation.

  • Real yield and real duration. The price moves with changes in real yields. Real duration applied to a change in nominal yields is wrong, because nominal yields also move with breakevens.
  • Worked comparison. Nominal 10-year at 4.30%, linker at 1.80% real: breakeven 2.50%. If inflation averages 3.0%, the linker returns about 1.80% + 3.0% = 4.8%, beating the nominal by roughly 0.5% a year.
  • Indexation lag. CPI is published with a delay, so indexation uses a lagged index. Linker carry swings with quarterly CPI prints and seasonal patterns.
  • Deflation protection. US TIPS repay at least their original face value at maturity.
  • Liquidity. Thinner than nominals, so linkers underperform in liquidity stress, even when inflation expectations are unchanged.
Floating rate notes, worked

A 4-year FRN pays 3-month BBSW + 90 bp (the quoted margin). The market now requires 110 bp (the discount margin). The note trades below par, by roughly the difference in margin times its spread duration:

Price ≈ 100 − (DM − QM) × spread duration ≈ 100 − 0.20% × ~3.8 ≈ 99.24

Its interest rate duration is about 0.25 years (to the next reset), so it barely moves when ACGB yields change. Its spread duration is nearly the full 4 years, so it moves with credit spreads. This is why bank FRNs and RMBS are the core of many Australian cash-plus and short-duration funds.

Callables, perpetuals and extension risk

Subordinated bank bonds are often "10 non-call 5": 10-year final maturity, callable at par after 5 years. The market assumes the issuer calls at the first date, so the bond is priced as a 5-year. If the issuer does not call (because refinancing is more expensive than keeping the bond), the bond extends, the spread duration jumps, and the price usually drops. That is extension risk.

The issuer holds the option, so the investor is short convexity: when spreads tighten the bond is called and your upside is capped; when spreads widen the bond extends and you hold it longer at the wrong price. Compare these bonds on yield to worst and OAS, and ask what the reset spread on non-call would be.

Breakeven inflation

Breakeven = nominal yield − real yield = expected inflation + inflation risk premium − liquidity premium Linker nominal return ≈ real yield + realised inflation

If realised inflation over the bond's life exceeds the breakeven, the linker outperforms the nominal bond. The inflation risk premium is what investors pay to be protected against inflation uncertainty; it pushes breakevens up. The liquidity premium on linkers pushes breakevens down, because linkers are harder to trade. In stress, linkers often cheapen relative to nominals even with no change in inflation expectations, as happened in 2008 and March 2020.

Inflation swaps

A zero-coupon inflation swap exchanges a fixed rate for realised inflation over the life of the swap, paid at maturity. The fixed rate is a clean market measure of inflation compensation, free of bond supply and repo effects. Comparing swap rates with bond breakevens reveals relative value between cash linkers and swaps.

Real yield drivers

  • The expected path of real policy rates and the neutral real rate.
  • Real term premium.
  • Supply of linkers and demand from pension funds and inflation-hedging investors.

Real yields move less than nominal yields for most moves: the real yield "beta" to nominal yields is typically below one, because part of each nominal move is a change in breakevens. Hedging a linker with nominal futures one-for-one on duration usually over-hedges.

You think breakevens are too low. Buy the linker and sell the nominal bond so that the position is insensitive to real yields and gains if breakevens widen.

Inflation carry and seasonality

A linker's carry over a month or a quarter includes the inflation accrual on its principal, set by the lagged CPI. If CPI has strong seasonal patterns (for example price rises in particular quarters), linker carry varies through the year. Breakeven trades held over a quarter with high seasonal inflation benefit from positive carry; the reverse costs money. Desks forecast the upcoming index prints to estimate carry.

What moves breakevens
  • Energy prices: short-dated breakevens track oil and petrol closely.
  • Central bank credibility: long-dated breakevens and 5y5y forward breakevens are watched as a gauge of anchored expectations.
  • Risk appetite and liquidity: linkers underperform in stress.
  • Supply: new linker issuance cheapens linkers.
  • Pension and LDI demand: strong in the UK long end.
Linkers in a portfolio

Linkers protect against inflation surprises, which matters most when stocks and nominal bonds fall together, as in 2022. They are a natural partial replacement for nominal duration when the risk is inflation rather than growth. The costs: lower liquidity, longer real duration than many investors expect, and underperformance in deflationary or liquidity shocks.

Credit spreads, losses and excess return

A credit position is a short put on the issuer's assets. The spread is the premium, and the question is always whether it pays for expected losses plus the risk of bad timing.

Expected loss and excess return

Expected loss = PD × LGD (LGD = 1 − recovery rate) Excess return ≈ Spread × t − SpreadDur × Δs − PD × LGD × t Breakeven widening = (Spread × t − expected loss) ÷ SpreadDur

Historically, investment-grade spreads have paid well above realised default losses. Most of the spread is risk premium and liquidity premium, not expected loss. That is the credit spread puzzle, and it is why a diversified IG allocation has earned positive excess returns over long periods despite painful drawdowns. In high yield, expected losses are a much larger share of the spread and the timing of the cycle dominates.

DTS: duration times spread

Spread changes tend to be proportional to the level of spreads: a bond at 400 bp moves more in basis points than one at 80 bp. Research by Ben Dor and co-authors at Lehman/Barclays (2007) made this the basis of DTS = spread duration × spread. DTS is a better measure of credit risk than spread duration alone, and many desks set credit limits in DTS.

Worked example: from spread to implied default rate

A 5-year BBB bond trades at 150 bp. Assume a 40% recovery (60% loss given default).

Implied annual default probability ≈ spread ÷ LGD = 1.50% ÷ 0.60 = 2.5%

Historical BBB default rates have been far below 2.5% a year. The gap is the credit risk premium plus the liquidity premium. The market-implied (risk-neutral) default rate is what you would have to believe to be indifferent; the difference between that and your real-world estimate is the expected excess return you are being offered.

Worked example: excess return with spread roll-down

Credit curves usually slope upward too. A 5-year bond at 120 bp with 4-year bonds from the same issuer at 105 bp will "roll" 15 bp tighter in a year if the curve is unchanged.

  1. Spread carry: +1.20%.
  2. Spread roll-down: 15 bp × spread duration at horizon (about 3.7) = +0.55%.
  3. Expected loss: say 0.15% a year, so −0.15%.
  4. Expected excess return with unchanged spreads: about +1.6%. Breakeven widening ≈ 1.6% ÷ 3.7 ≈ 43 bp.

Roll-down is often the difference between an attractive and an unattractive credit position, especially in steep sections of the curve like the 3- to 7-year part of IG.

Spread duration versus DTS, worked

Two bonds, both with spread duration 5. Bond A trades at 80 bp (DTS = 4.0). Bond B trades at 400 bp (DTS = 20).

In a risk-off move, spreads tend to widen by a similar percentage, say 10%. A widens 8 bp and loses 0.40%. B widens 40 bp and loses 2.0%. Spread duration says the two bonds have the same risk; DTS says B has five times the risk, which matches experience. Portfolio DTS relative to the benchmark is the best single number for active credit risk.

Allocating across credit sectors
  • Beta. HY moves roughly several times as much as IG in spread terms. A small HY allocation can dominate credit risk.
  • Financials versus non-financials. Bank senior is short, liquid and tightly regulated; subordinated bank debt carries regulatory risk. Non-financials give more idiosyncratic dispersion.
  • Crossover (BBB/BB). Where fallen angels and rising stars create forced buying and selling and therefore opportunity.
  • Credit index hedges. CDX IG, CDX HY, iTraxx Main and Crossover let you change credit beta in minutes, leaving your bond selection intact.
  • Liquidity budget. Know what share of the book you could sell in a week at a sensible cost.
Liquidity in credit

Corporate bonds trade over the counter through dealers whose balance sheets are limited. In calm markets the bid–offer on a liquid IG bond may be a few basis points; in stress, it can widen many times over and some bonds do not trade at all. New issues are the most liquid for a few months, then liquidity fades. Measures such as bid–ask spread, trade counts and liquidity cost scores help. Holding a buffer of highly liquid assets (government bonds, bank FRNs) means you are not a forced seller of credit at the worst moment.

Common mistakes
  • Comparing G-spreads across markets with very different swap spreads.
  • Using Z-spread for callables instead of OAS.
  • Treating spread duration as the only measure of credit risk. Use DTS.
  • Reaching for yield late in the cycle. The last 20 bp of spread in CCCs rarely pays for the tail.
  • Assuming you can sell in stress at the marks you see in calm markets.

Structural subordination matters as much as contractual ranking. Debt issued by a holding company ranks behind all creditors of the operating subsidiaries where the assets and cash flows sit, unless the subsidiaries guarantee it. Always check which entity issued the bond and which entities guarantee it.

In principle, the absolute priority rule pays senior claims in full before juniors get anything. In practice, negotiated restructurings (US Chapter 11, Australian voluntary administration and schemes of arrangement) often give juniors something to secure agreement, and legal costs and priority claims come off the top.

Covenants

  • Maintenance covenants are tested regularly (for example, net leverage below 5.0x each quarter). A breach gives lenders a seat at the table early. Standard in private credit.
  • Incurrence covenants only bite when the borrower takes an action, such as raising debt or paying a dividend. Standard in high yield bonds.
  • Covenant-lite loans have no maintenance tests. Most US and European broadly syndicated loans are now cov-lite, so problems surface later and recoveries tend to be lower.
  • Liability management exercises (LMEs): uptiers, drop-downs of assets to unrestricted subsidiaries, and non-pro-rata exchanges. Loose documents let a majority group of lenders or the sponsor move ahead of other creditors. Document quality is now a core part of loan and HY analysis.

Recovery and the fulcrum

In a restructuring, the enterprise value of the business is distributed down the stack. The fulcrum security is the tranche where value runs out: it is partly covered, and its holders typically receive the new equity. Distressed investors buy the fulcrum. Historical averages, as a rough guide, show first lien loans recovering well above half of par, senior unsecured bonds well below half, and subordinated debt less again. The spread between these outcomes is why loans and bonds of the same issuer trade at very different spreads.

Secured leverage = secured debt ÷ EBITDA Total leverage = total debt ÷ EBITDA Loan-to-value = debt through a tranche ÷ enterprise value

Bank capital structure

Banks have their own stack, set by regulation: CET1 equity, then AT1, then Tier 2, then senior unsecured and deposits. After 2008, regulators designed AT1 and Tier 2 to absorb losses at the point of non-viability, before taxpayers. Australian major banks must also hold additional loss-absorbing capacity, which they meet largely with Tier 2. For bank credit, the regulator's resolution powers matter more than bankruptcy law.

Worked example: how the fulcrum moves

A company has EBITDA of $100m, $400m of first lien debt and $200m of senior unsecured notes: 6.0x leverage.

  1. Trading slumps and EBITDA falls to $70m. The market values the business at 6.0x, an enterprise value of $420m.
  2. After $20m of administration costs, $400m is left for creditors. First lien recovers 100%; the unsecured notes recover nothing. The first lien is (just) the fulcrum.
  3. If buyers will only pay 5.5x ($385m), $365m is left: first lien recovers 91% and takes the equity; the notes are wiped out.
  4. If EBITDA recovers to $90m at 6.0x ($540m), $520m is distributable: first lien is paid in full and the notes recover 60%. The notes are now the fulcrum and would receive the equity in a restructuring.

Small changes in the valuation multiple move the fulcrum. That is why distressed investors spend so much time on the enterprise value, not just the default probability.

Liability management exercises

Since around 2020, stressed borrowers in the US and Europe have used loose loan documents to raise new money at the expense of existing lenders:

  • Uptier: a majority lender group provides new money and exchanges its loans into new debt that ranks ahead of the loans held by non-participants.
  • Drop-down: valuable assets (intellectual property, a subsidiary) are moved to an unrestricted subsidiary that can raise debt secured on them, outside the original lenders' collateral.
  • Double-dip and other structures that give new lenders two claims on the same value.

Lenders now negotiate protective clauses (often named after the cases that prompted them, such as "J.Crew" and "Serta" blockers). For a PM, the practical lesson is that the same headline seniority can mean very different recoveries depending on the document.

Reading a term sheet: what to check
  • Issuer and guarantors: which entities owe you money, and where are the assets?
  • Ranking and security: secured on what? Is there a super senior revolver ahead of you?
  • Debt baskets: how much more debt can be raised, and ranking where?
  • Restricted payments: how much cash can leave to shareholders?
  • EBITDA definition: generous add-backs make leverage covenants meaningless.
  • Change of control: high yield bonds usually include a put at 101 if the company is sold.
  • Call protection: non-call period, make-whole, soft call at 101.
Recovery in practice

Recovery is measured two ways: the trading price of the debt about 30 days after default (what rating agencies often use), and the ultimate recovery after the restructuring completes, which can take years. Recoveries are higher for asset-heavy businesses with saleable collateral and lower for asset-light ones such as software, services or retail, where value sits in the going concern. Recoveries also fall in default waves, because many businesses are sold or restructured at once into a weak market.

Bank resolution

Banks are not wound up through ordinary insolvency when they fail. The regulator (APRA in Australia) has powers to intervene earlier, and bank capital instruments contain clauses that let them be converted to equity or written off at the point of non-viability. The Credit Suisse rescue in 2023, where AT1 was written off while shareholders kept some value, showed that the contract and the regulator's powers can override the textbook hierarchy. For bank credit, read the instrument terms and understand the local resolution regime.

Relative value toolkit

  • Spread versus the curve for its rating and sector. Fit a spread curve against duration; bonds above it are cheap, below it rich. Then ask why: there is usually a reason.

Market valuation levels

At the asset allocation level, the question is whether credit as a whole is paid enough. Worked example: spread cushion per unit of risk

Two bonds from the same issuer:

  • 3-year bond at 100 bp, spread duration 2.8. Breakeven widening = 100 ÷ 2.8 = 36 bp.
  • 10-year bond at 150 bp, spread duration 7.9. Breakeven widening = 150 ÷ 7.9 = 19 bp.

The 10-year pays more spread, but its cushion against widening is about half as big. If you expect volatility, the shorter bond is better value per unit of risk; if you expect spreads to tighten, the longer bond gains more. This is the credit equivalent of the breakeven analysis in module 18.

Building a fair value model

A simple cross-sectional model regresses spreads on the characteristics that should explain them:

Spread ≈ a + b × spread duration + c × leverage + rating dummies + sector dummies + liquidity (issue size)

The residual is the rich/cheap signal. Bonds that are cheap for a reason (upcoming supply, weak documentation, a downgrade you have not spotted) will stay cheap, so treat residuals as questions, not answers. The best trades are cheap bonds where your fundamental work says the reason is temporary.

Credit curves and CDS
  • Issuer curve shape. Healthy issuers have upward-sloping credit curves. When default risk becomes near-term, curves flatten and then invert, as short bonds start to price near-term default.
  • Credit curve trades. Buy the 5-year, sell 10-year protection, spread-duration-weighted, to express a view on curve shape without a directional credit view.
  • CDS–bond basis. Negative basis (CDS below bond spread) often appears in stress when balance sheets are scarce; buying the bond and protection can lock in the gap, subject to funding and counterparty risk.
Government bond rich/cheap

Rates desks run the same logic on government curves. Fit a smooth curve through all bonds and look at each bond's residual yield. Bonds trade rich or cheap because of futures deliverability (bonds in the futures basket tend to trade rich), repo specialness, auction cycles (bonds cheapen before a tender and recover after), and index events. Asset swap spreads of individual bonds are another lens.

Is credit expensive? Adjusting for index composition

Comparing today's index spread with its history can mislead, because the index changes. The global IG index today has a higher share of BBB and longer duration than twenty years ago; both push spreads wider for the same risk appetite. Compare like with like: rating-matched and duration-matched spreads, or spread per unit of duration. Also look at all-in yield: when yields are high, yield-driven buyers (insurers, retirees, annuity providers) absorb supply and keep spreads tight for longer than history suggests.

Structural models

Merton (1974) treated a company's equity as a call option on its assets, with the face value of debt as the strike. If asset value falls below debt at maturity, shareholders walk away and creditors take the assets. Debt is then a risk-free bond minus a put option on the assets.

Equity = V·N(d₁) − F·e^(−rT)·N(d₂) d₁ = [ln(V/F) + (r + σ²/2)T] ÷ (σ√T), d₂ = d₁ − σ√T Risk-neutral PD = N(−d₂) Distance to default ≈ d₂ (standard deviations from default)

The model links credit to equity: falling share prices and rising equity volatility raise default risk. Moody's KMV built a commercial default model on this idea. It tends to understate short-dated spreads, because in the basic model a firm cannot default suddenly.

Reduced-form (hazard rate) models

These models treat default as a random event arriving at a rate λ (the hazard rate), calibrated to market spreads rather than balance sheets.

Survival to t: Q(t) = e^(−λt) Spread ≈ λ × (1 − Recovery) the "credit triangle" Default probability over t = 1 − e^(−λt)

A 200 bp spread with 40% recovery implies λ ≈ 3.33% a year and a 5-year default probability of about 15.4% under risk-neutral measure. Real-world default probabilities are lower; the gap is the credit risk premium.

Credit default swaps

  • The protection buyer pays a regular premium; the seller pays par minus recovery if a credit event (bankruptcy, failure to pay, and for some contracts restructuring) occurs.
  • Contracts trade with standard coupons (100 bp or 500 bp in North America, with other standard levels in Europe) and an upfront payment that makes up the difference between the market spread and the coupon.
  • After a credit event, an auction sets a single recovery price for settlement. A determinations committee rules on whether a credit event has occurred.
  • CDS lets you short credit, hedge a specific issuer, or take credit risk without funding a bond.

Worked example: Merton and the equity market

Assets $1,000m, debt $700m due in 5 years, asset volatility 25%, risk-free rate 4%. The model gives equity of about $462m and debt of about $538m, against a risk-free value of $573m for the same promised payment. If asset volatility rises to 35%, equity rises to about $506m (the option is worth more), debt falls to about $494m and the spread roughly doubles to about 300 bp. Equity holders gain from risk-taking at creditors' expense, which is why lenders use covenants.

Risk-neutral versus real-world default probabilities

Market-implied (risk-neutral) default probabilities are always higher than historical default rates, because spreads include compensation for risk and liquidity. For pricing and hedging, use risk-neutral probabilities, since they are consistent with market prices. For expected return and CME work, use real-world estimates. Confusing the two is a common error: a model that uses risk-neutral probabilities to forecast losses will make credit look unattractive at every spread level.

Using CDS in a portfolio
  • Macro hedges: buy protection on CDX HY or iTraxx Crossover to reduce credit beta quickly without selling bonds.
  • Single-name hedges: protect a concentrated position while waiting to sell.
  • Basis trades: exploit gaps between bond spreads and CDS.
  • Curve trades: buy 5-year and sell 10-year protection to express a view on an issuer's credit curve.
  • Risks: counterparty exposure (mostly mitigated by central clearing for indices), basis risk between CDS and bonds, and contract definitions (whether restructuring counts, which obligations are deliverable).

Debt sustainability

The core equation of public debt: the debt-to-GDP ratio rises when the interest rate on debt exceeds nominal growth, unless the government runs a primary surplus (a surplus before interest payments).

Δd ≈ (r − g) ÷ (1 + g) × d − primary balance d = debt / GDP, r = average nominal interest rate, g = nominal GDP growth
  • When r < g, debt ratios can fall even with small primary deficits. That was the case for many advanced economies in the 2010s.
  • When r > g, stabilising debt requires a primary surplus, which is politically hard.
  • Average interest cost adjusts slowly as debt is refinanced, so a rise in market yields feeds through over years. Short average maturity speeds this up.

Willingness to pay and restructuring

Sovereigns cannot be liquidated. Defaults are usually resolved through negotiated exchanges of old bonds for new ones with lower coupons, longer maturities or reduced face value, often alongside an IMF program. Collective action clauses (CACs) let a supermajority of bondholders bind all holders, limiting holdouts. Restructurings can take years, and recoveries vary widely with the country's situation and the creditor mix (bondholders, official lenders, and increasingly non-Paris Club lenders such as China).

Hard currency versus local currency

  • Hard currency (USD): spread over US Treasuries; driven by sovereign credit, US rates and global risk appetite. Benchmark family: J.P. Morgan EMBI.
  • Local currency: exposure to local rates and the currency; the currency usually dominates volatility. Benchmark family: GBI-EM. A country can always pay local debt by printing, so the risk shows up as inflation and depreciation rather than default.
  • EM corporates: often priced relative to their sovereign; the sovereign ceiling limits ratings for many issuers.

Advanced economy sovereigns

Countries that borrow in their own currency with credible central banks (the US, UK, Japan, Australia) do not face involuntary default in the usual sense. Their risk shows up in term premium, the currency and inflation. Market stress can still be severe: the UK gilt crisis in 2022 followed an unfunded fiscal announcement. Australian states

State credit depends on each state's budget, its infrastructure program, and the backdrop of Commonwealth support through GST distribution and grants. Ratings agencies watch debt relative to revenue and interest costs relative to revenue. EM crisis signals

Securitisation: RMBS, ABS, CMBS and CLOs

Securitisation turns a pool of loans into tranches with very different risk. Understanding how losses and prepayments flow through the structure is the whole game.

By the end of this module you can

  • Compare the main sectors: agency MBS, Australian RMBS, CMBS, ABS and CLOs.
  • Use CPR, CDR, severity, WAL, OC and IC tests fluently.
  • Explain prepayment risk and why MBS are negatively convex.

How a deal works

  1. An originator (bank, non-bank lender, CLO manager) sells a pool of loans to a bankruptcy-remote special purpose vehicle.
  2. The SPV issues notes in tranches, from senior AAA down to an equity or residual piece, usually kept by the originator.
  3. Collections from the pool pay interest and principal through a waterfall, seniors first.
  4. Losses hit the most junior tranche first and work upward.

Credit enhancement

  • Subordination: junior tranches absorb losses before the senior. The AAA tranche's subordination is its main protection.
  • Excess spread: the pool earns more interest than the notes pay. That surplus absorbs losses each period before any tranche is hit.
  • Overcollateralisation (OC): the pool balance exceeds the notes.
  • Reserve funds and liquidity facilities, and in Australian RMBS, lenders mortgage insurance on high-LVR loans.
  • Triggers: if losses, arrears or OC breach limits, cash is diverted to pay down seniors (switching from pro-rata to sequential pay).

Worked example: a loss waterfall

A $1,000m prime RMBS pool is tranched: AAA 90%, AA 3%, A 2%, BBB 1.5%, BB 1%, B 1%, unrated 1.5%.

  1. Pool losses reach 2.2%, which is $22m, after excess spread has been used.
  2. The unrated tranche ($15m) is wiped out.
  3. The remaining $7m hits the B tranche ($10m): a 70% loss.
  4. Everything from BB upward is untouched. The AAA would need losses above 10% before it lost a dollar.

This is why senior tranches of prime pools rarely lose money: it takes pool losses far beyond historical experience. It is also why the equity and junior tranches are so sensitive to the loss assumption.

Excess spread, worked

A non-bank RMBS pool earns BBSW + 2.4% on its loans. The notes pay a weighted average of BBSW + 1.1%, and servicing, trustee and other costs are 0.4%.

Excess spread = 2.4% − 1.1% − 0.4% = 0.9% a year

Each year, the first 0.9% of losses is absorbed before any note is written down. Surplus is released to the residual holder (often the originator), which gives the originator an incentive to lend carefully. Excess spread shrinks as the pool pays down and as higher-yielding loans prepay first, so it is less protective late in a deal's life.

Australian RMBS specifics
  • Prime versus non-conforming. Prime pools are full-doc, lower LVR loans; non-conforming pools include self-employed, credit-impaired or specialist borrowers, with higher yields and more subordination.
  • Lenders mortgage insurance on high-LVR loans provides another layer of protection.
  • Key metrics: 30+ and 90+ day arrears, LVR distribution, investor versus owner-occupier share, interest-only share, geographic concentration.
  • Paydown: sequential at first, then pro-rata after a step-down date if performance tests are met.
  • Calls: issuers normally call at the first call date (or clean-up call). Deals are priced to call, so a missed call creates extension risk.
  • Notes are floating rate off BBSW, so they carry spread risk but very little rate duration.
CLOs in more detail
  • Life cycle: a ramp-up period, a non-call period (often about two years), a reinvestment period (often four to five years) where the manager can trade loans, then amortisation.
  • Quality tests: weighted average rating factor, diversity score, and a limit on CCC-rated loans (commonly 7.5%). CCC holdings above the limit are carried at a haircut in the OC test.
  • Coverage tests: OC (par of collateral ÷ par of notes) and IC (interest received ÷ interest owed) for each tranche. A breach diverts cash from equity and junior notes to pay down senior notes.
  • Equity is levered roughly ten times to the loan pool: high income when defaults are low, very sensitive to defaults and loan prices.
  • Track record: senior CLO tranches have a strong record of avoiding losses through past default cycles, which is why AAA CLOs have become a mainstream holding for banks and insurers.
Agency MBS and prepayment

US agency MBS carry government-backed guarantees, so the risk is almost entirely prepayment. Borrowers can refinance whenever rates fall, so prepayment speeds follow an S-curve against the refinancing incentive (their mortgage rate minus current rates). Pools that have already been through a refinancing wave prepay more slowly afterwards (burnout).

  • When rates fall, speeds rise and WAL shortens: holders get cash back when reinvestment yields are low (contraction).
  • When rates rise, speeds fall and WAL lengthens: holders are stuck with below-market coupons (extension).
  • MBS investors and servicers hedge this negative convexity by buying or selling Treasuries and swaps, which can amplify rate moves ("convexity hedging flows").

MBS are valued with OAS models that simulate rate paths and prepayments. Most trading happens in the forward TBA market, where the dollar roll gives an implied financing rate.

Due diligence questions for structured credit
  • Who originated the loans, and how have their previous deals performed?
  • What are the default and severity assumptions behind the rating, and how far are they from stress history?
  • What triggers exist, and how close is the deal to breaching them?
  • How much subordination and excess spread remain today, not at issue?
  • What happens to the tranche if the call is not exercised?
  • How liquid is the tranche, and who else owns it?

Valuation and the smoothing problem

Private loans are marked quarterly using models and comparables (fair value Level 3), not market prices. Reported volatility is therefore far lower than the true economic risk, and correlation with public markets looks lower than it is. For portfolio construction, de-smooth returns or proxy the risk with leveraged loans or high yield. Otherwise the optimiser will over-allocate.

Warning signs in a portfolio

  • A rising share of income paid in kind (PIK) rather than cash.
  • Frequent amendments and covenant resets, and "amend and extend" to avoid defaults.
  • Marks held near par on loans to companies whose public peers have sold off.
  • Concentration in one sector, sponsor or vintage.
For wealth portfolios

How a sponsor-backed deal is structured
  • A private equity sponsor buys a company, funding roughly half with equity and half with debt.
  • The direct lender provides a unitranche or senior loan, usually at several times EBITDA, with a loan-to-value well below 100% because the sponsor's equity sits beneath.
  • Maintenance covenants are set with headroom (commonly a 25% to 35% EBITDA cushion) and tested quarterly.
  • Lenders also negotiate information rights, amendment fees and call protection.
  • If the company underperforms, the lender negotiates directly with the sponsor: equity injection, fee, higher margin, or a switch to PIK. This control is the main advantage over syndicated loans.
The smoothing problem, and de-smoothing

Quarterly marks based on models and comparables change slowly. A fund's reported return series therefore shows low volatility and low correlation with public markets, and portfolio optimisers will treat it as a near-risk-free asset with a high return.

A common fix is to de-smooth: assume reported returns are a blend of this quarter's true return and last quarter's reported return, and back out the true series:

r_true ≈ (r_reported − φ × r_reported,previous) ÷ (1 − φ) φ = first-order autocorrelation of reported returns

De-smoothed volatility is usually close to that of leveraged loans or high yield. Use that for risk budgets, not the reported number.

Real estate debt in Australia

Bank capital rules and appetite have made banks more selective in construction and development lending, and non-bank lenders have filled the gap. Loans include land and construction finance, bridging finance and investment loans on completed buildings.

  • Construction risk: builder solvency, cost overruns and delays.
  • Sales risk: pre-sales settle only if buyers can still finance and valuations hold.
  • Position: first mortgage versus second mortgage or preferred equity makes a large difference to recovery.
  • Valuation: a property valuation drives the LVR, but valuations lag falling markets.
Evergreen and semi-liquid funds

Wealth clients usually access private credit through open-ended funds that offer periodic redemptions, commonly capped at a small share of NAV per quarter, with gates when requests exceed the cap. Check:

  • How the redemption limits compare with the loans' natural repayment rate.
  • Whether the fund holds a liquid sleeve, and how much it dilutes returns.
  • How new money is priced relative to NAV, and whether marks are independently reviewed.
  • Whether distributions are covered by cash income or supported by PIK and capital.
Manager due diligence checklist
  1. Origination: sponsor relationships, share of deals led versus participated, selectivity.
  2. Underwriting: leverage and LTV at entry, covenant package, documentation standards.
  3. Track record: losses through a full cycle, including restructured loans and those still marked near par.
  4. Portfolio: sector, borrower and vintage concentration, share of PIK and non-accruals.
  5. Valuation: policy, frequency, third-party review, how marks compare with public comparables.
  6. Fees and conflicts: management fee basis (gross or net assets), performance fee hurdle, who keeps borrower fees, related-party transactions.

Worked example: convertible metrics

A $1,000 convertible converts into 20 shares (conversion price $50). The shares trade at $42, the convertible at $1,030, and the straight bond value is $900.

  1. Parity = 20 × $42 = $840.
  2. Conversion premium = ($1,030 − $840) ÷ $840 ≈ 22.6%.

Convertible risks that surprise investors
  • The bond floor is not fixed: when the share price collapses, credit quality usually weakens and the floor falls too.
  • Issuer call provisions can force conversion early, cutting off the option's time value.
  • Dividend protection and takeover clauses matter for the option value.
  • Liquidity is thin, and convertible arbitrage funds can be forced sellers at the same time.

Building blocks for fixed income CMEs

Two complementary lenses. The building-block lens asks what each asset should pay in equilibrium. The horizon-return lens asks what a specific bond will earn over the next year under a scenario.

By the end of this module you can
  • Build an expected return from building blocks: real rate, inflation, term, credit and liquidity premia.
  • Decompose a one-year horizon return into carry, roll, rate, spread, loss and currency effects.
  • Produce multi-year return estimates anchored on starting yield, with mean reversion.

Lens 1: the building-block approach

Start from the risk-free short rate and add a premium for each risk the asset carries.

E(R) = real short rate + expected inflation → nominal short rate + term premium (includes inflation risk premium) + credit spread − expected default losses → credit risk premium + liquidity premium
  • Short-term default-free rate. Anchor on the policy rate path, not today's cash rate. Use the neutral rate for long horizons.
  • Term premium. Larger for longer maturities; varies over time (module 3). Tends to be higher when inflation is uncertain and bonds are positively correlated with equities.
  • Credit premium. The spread minus expected losses. What is left pays for default uncertainty, downgrade risk, and the fact that credit losses cluster in recessions when investors can least bear them.
  • Liquidity premium. Higher for small, infrequently traded issues, private credit and non-benchmark bonds. Near zero for on-the-run government bonds.

The best single predictor: starting yield

For high-quality bonds, the starting yield of a portfolio is a strong predictor of its return over a horizon near its duration. Over a horizon equal to the Macaulay duration, a one-off parallel shift is roughly offset: capital losses from higher yields are recovered through reinvestment at those higher yields. So the first question in any fixed income CME is "what yield are we starting from?", and the adjustments come after.

Practical adjustments

  • Mean reversion. For long horizons, move spreads and term premia part of the way toward long-run averages rather than holding today's level.
  • Scenario weighting. Build base, bull and bear cases with explicit probabilities. The distribution matters as much as the mean, especially for credit, where losses are skewed.
  • Callables and MBS. Use option-adjusted yield and spread; YTM overstates the return.
  • Currency-hedged returns. For offshore bonds, the hedge adds or subtracts the short-rate differential (module 22).
  • Consistency. Your inflation assumption must be the same in the bond, equity and real asset CMEs. Committees notice.

Worked example: a one-year government bond forecast

A 10-year government bond: coupon income 4.30%, modified duration 8.2, convexity 80. The curve implies roll-down of 0.25% over the year. Your base case is a 20 bp rise in yields.

  1. Rolling yield: 4.30% + 0.25% = 4.55%.
  2. Duration effect: −8.2 × 0.20% = −1.64%.
  3. Convexity: ½ × 80 × 0.002² = +0.02%.
  4. Expected return ≈ 2.93%.

Now compare with cash at 3.6%: the bond underperforms in your base case. The breakeven yield rise is (4.55% − 3.60%) ÷ 8.2 ≈ 12 bp. Put the scenario into the horizon-return lab and compare.

Multi-year forecasts: the starting yield rule

For strategic CMEs (5 to 10 years), yield changes are spread over the horizon and reinvestment at new yields offsets price losses. A useful approximation for the annualised return over horizon H when yields move by Δy:

Annual return ≈ y₀ + Δy × (1 − D ÷ H)

With D = 6, H = 10, y₀ = 4.3% and a 50 bp rise: 4.3% + 0.5% × 0.4 = 4.5%. Rising yields add to 10-year returns once the horizon exceeds duration. At H = D the return equals the starting yield. At H = 1 with D = 6, the same move costs 2.5%. This is why long-horizon investors should welcome higher yields, and why short-horizon CMEs depend heavily on the yield forecast.

Credit CMEs

For credit, start from spread, then subtract losses and adjust for spread mean reversion:

E(excess return) ≈ spread − expected loss − SpreadDur × E(Δspread)/H
  • IG: expected losses are small, so the spread is mostly premium. The main question is spread change.
  • HY: estimate default rates through the cycle (not today's low rate) and recovery rates for the likely mix of secured and unsecured debt.
  • Loans and private credit: use floating base-rate forecasts plus margin, less losses, less fees.

  • Give ranges and scenarios, not single points, and show how the ranking of asset classes changes.

Carry and roll-down

Carry is what a position earns from income less its funding cost. Roll-down is the price gain as a bond ages and its yield falls down an upward-sloping curve. Together they are the return if the curve does not move. The model below computes both for each tenor from your working curve, using zero-coupon bonds so the maths is exact.

The breakeven is the most useful number here: it tells you how far yields can rise before the position loses money. A tenor with high carry and roll per unit of duration is where the curve pays you to sit.

A bullet concentrates holdings near one maturity. A barbell splits them between short and long maturities with the same duration. A 5-year zero at 4.00%; the 4-year point on the curve is 3.85%. Horizon one year, curve unchanged.

  1. Carry: 4.00% × 1 year = 4.00%.
  2. Roll-down: the bond becomes a 4-year and its yield falls 15 bp. Price gain ≈ 4 × 0.15% = 0.60%.
  3. Total ≈ 4.60%. If funded at a 3.60% repo rate, the funded return is 1.00%.
  4. Breakeven: the funded position loses money only if the 4-year yield ends more than about 1.00% ÷ 4 = 25 bp above its current 3.85%.

Rank every tenor by carry and roll per unit of duration. The best tenors are where a steep part of the curve meets a moderate duration, often the 3- to 7-year sector.

Butterflies and relative value on the curve

A butterfly sells the belly and buys the wings (or the reverse), sized so the trade is neutral to parallel moves. The fly spread (2 × belly yield − wing yields) measures how cheap the belly is.

  • 50/50 DV01 fly: each wing carries half the belly's DV01. Neutral to level, exposed to slope.
  • Regression or PCA fly: wing weights chosen so the trade is also neutral to slope, isolating curvature.
  • Look at the fly's carry and roll, its z-score against history, and what is driving it (supply in the belly, futures deliverability, central bank purchases).
Duration timing: signals desks use
  • Valuation: real yields relative to estimates of the neutral real rate; term premium estimates.
  • Carry: steep curves pay you to be long; inverted curves charge you.
  • Momentum: trend-following signals on yields work over months.
  • Macro surprises: growth and inflation data relative to consensus.
  • Policy: what the central bank is signalling versus what is priced.
  • Positioning: surveys and futures positioning show when a view is crowded.

No single signal is reliable. Many desks combine several into a score and size duration positions accordingly, with firm limits.

Options in curve strategies
  • Buying receiver swaptions or bond calls: gain if yields fall sharply, lose only the premium. Adds convexity.
  • Selling options (covered calls on bonds, selling payers): earn premium in range-bound markets, give up convexity.
  • Implied versus realised volatility decides whether buying or selling convexity is cheap. A barbell versus bullet choice is really a convexity trade, so compare the barbell's yield give-up with what the same convexity would cost in options.

Normal volatility and the Bachelier model

Rates desks quote volatility in basis points per year (normal volatility), because rate changes are closer to normally distributed in bp than in percentage terms, and rates can go negative. Lognormal (Black) volatility, quoted as a percentage of the rate, was the older convention.

Payer = A × [ (F − K)·N(d) + σ√T·n(d) ] Receiver = A × [ (K − F)·N(−d) + σ√T·n(d) ] d = (F − K) ÷ (σ√T) F = forward swap rate, K = strike, σ = normal vol, A = annuity (PV01 of the swap) At the money: price ≈ A × σ√T × 0.4

A useful rule: normal vol ÷ √252 is the expected daily move in bp. 100 bp a year of vol implies about 6.3 bp a day. If you are long an at-the-money straddle, you make money if realised daily moves exceed that on average.

The volatility surface

Swaption vol is quoted as a grid: expiry (1 month to 10 years or more) by tenor of the underlying swap (1 to 30 years), plus skew across strikes. The ICE BofA MOVE index summarises implied volatility on US Treasuries, the rates equivalent of the VIX. Vol tends to rise when policy is uncertain, and short-expiry vol spikes around central bank meetings and data.

OAS and interest rate trees

To value a bond whose cash flows depend on rates, you need a model of how rates might evolve:

  1. Build a tree (binomial or trinomial) or run Monte Carlo paths of short rates, using a model such as Ho–Lee, Black–Derman–Toy or Hull–White.
  2. Calibrate it to today's yield curve and to market volatility (usually swaptions).
  3. Value the bond backwards through the tree, applying the option rule at each node (the issuer calls when the bond's value exceeds the call price; mortgages prepay according to a prepayment model).
  4. Find the constant spread added to every rate in the tree that makes the model price equal the market price. That is the OAS.
  5. Shift the curve up and down, reprice holding OAS constant, and you have effective duration and convexity.
Callable bond = straight bond − call option (held by issuer) Putable bond = straight bond + put option (held by investor) Option cost = Z-spread − OAS

Higher volatility makes the call option more valuable, so a callable bond's price falls and its OAS falls for a given market price. OAS is only as good as the model and the volatility input behind it.

The Greeks for rates options
  • Delta: sensitivity to the forward rate. An ATM payer has a delta of about 0.5 of the underlying swap's DV01.
  • Gamma: the rate of change of delta. Long options are long gamma: they gain from large moves.
  • Vega: sensitivity to a 1 bp change in normal vol. Largest for ATM, long-expiry options.
  • Theta: daily time decay. Long gamma costs theta; the trade-off is realised versus implied vol.

Delta-hedging a long option position daily earns roughly ½ × gamma × (realised move² − implied move²). That is the precise sense in which being long options is a bet on realised volatility exceeding implied.

Negative convexity in MBS, seen through options

An agency MBS holder is long a bond and short a prepayment option to every borrower. When rates fall, borrowers exercise. The MBS holder is therefore short volatility. MBS spreads tend to widen when rate volatility rises, and large holders (banks, the Fed in the past, REITs) delta-hedge by buying or selling duration as rates move, which can amplify the move. Hedging MBS with swaptions buys back some of the volatility they are short.

Using options in a fixed income portfolio
  • Tail hedges: buy out-of-the-money receivers or payers to protect against a sharp move in the direction you fear.
  • Yield enhancement: sell covered calls on bond futures or sell payers against long duration. You earn premium but give up upside or add risk in a sell-off.
  • Expressing asymmetric views: if a central bank cut is 90% priced, an option on the rarer outcome can be cheap.
  • Comparing barbells to options: a barbell's yield give-up buys convexity. If options offer the same convexity more cheaply, use options.

Liability-driven investing

When there are known future payments (pensions, insurance claims, a target date), the goal is to fund them with minimal risk. The key measure is the surplus: assets minus liabilities, both marked to market.

Immunising a single liability

  1. Macaulay duration of assets equals the liability's horizon.
  2. Present value of assets is at least the present value of the liability.
  3. Minimise convexity (cash flow dispersion) around the horizon, to limit exposure to non-parallel shifts.

Immunising multiple liabilities

  1. Match present values.
  2. Match BPV (money duration), so the surplus is insensitive to small parallel moves.
  3. Asset convexity must exceed liability convexity, but as little as possible, to limit twist risk.

Pension schemes often do not hedge 100% of liability interest rate risk. The hedge ratio (asset BPV ÷ liability BPV) is a policy decision that trades off surplus volatility against the return-seeking assets the fund needs. Module 22 computes the overlay.

Total return and absolute return

Total-return and unconstrained funds drop the index constraint and manage duration inside a band (often −2 to +8 years). Their return comes mostly from duration timing, curve, credit selection and relative value across countries. The main risk is that "unconstrained" becomes a hidden long-credit, long-equity-beta fund. Watch the correlation of returns with equities.

In a wealth OCIO seat

Private wealth mandates sit between these worlds: the benchmark is usually a composite index, but clients experience returns in absolute terms, care about income and liquidity, and hold fixed income as the defensive sleeve. So the defensive role (negative correlation with equities in a sell-off) often matters more than squeezing out tracking error. That is a term premium and stock–bond correlation question, which connects straight back to module 3.

Worked example: immunising a single liability

A client needs $10m in 7 years. Yields are 4% (annual).

  1. PV of the liability: 10 ÷ 1.04⁷ = $7.60m. That is the minimum portfolio value needed.
  2. Build a portfolio with Macaulay duration of 7 years, for example a mix of 5- and 10-year bonds, with cash flows clustered near year 7 to keep dispersion low.
  3. If yields rise, prices fall but coupons reinvest at higher rates; if yields fall, the reverse. For a parallel shift the two effects roughly cancel at the 7-year horizon.
  4. Rebalance periodically: duration falls more slowly than time passes, so the portfolio drifts away from the horizon.

A 7-year zero-coupon bond would immunise perfectly with zero dispersion. Immunisation with coupon bonds is an approximation to that.

Worked example: matching multiple liabilities by BPV

A pension has liabilities with PV $500m and duration 12, so liability BPV = $500m × 12 × 0.0001 = $600,000 per bp. Its bond portfolio is $300m at duration 6: BPV $180,000. Gap: $420,000 per bp.

  • A 100% hedge needs $420,000 of extra BPV: for example, receiving fixed on about $525m notional of a 10-year swap with duration 8.
  • A 60% hedge ratio targets liability BPV × 0.6 = $360,000, needing $180,000 of additional BPV.
  • Asset convexity should slightly exceed liability convexity so the surplus benefits from large moves in either direction.

The UK gilt crisis in September 2022 showed the other side: leveraged LDI funds using swaps and repo faced large collateral calls as yields rose sharply, forcing gilt sales until the Bank of England intervened. Collateral buffers are part of LDI design.

Where enhanced indexing tracking error comes from
  • Small duration and key rate mismatches.
  • Sector and quality weight differences.
  • Issuer concentration: holding fewer names than the index means each default or downgrade matters more.
  • Transaction costs and cash drag.
  • Index rebalancing: new issues enter at month-end, and bonds leave as they fall below one year or are downgraded.
Fixed income in wealth portfolios

Private clients usually hold fixed income for income, capital stability and diversification against equities. That shapes the design:

  • Benchmarks: often a blend of the AusBond Composite and a global aggregate index hedged to AUD.
  • Ladders and target-maturity funds: give clients visible maturities and cash flows.
  • Income focus: clients see coupons, so higher-yielding sleeves (hybrids, private credit) are popular. Make sure the risk budget, not the yield, drives the allocation.
  • Liquidity: semi-liquid products must be sized against the client's own liquidity needs.
  • Communication: private bankers need clear explanations of why bonds fell when yields rose, and why that means higher future returns.

A tactical asset allocation process for fixed income

  1. Signals: valuation, carry, momentum and macro (growth and inflation surprises, policy stance), each converted to a standardised score (z-score).
  2. Combination: weight the signals, often equally or by long-run reliability. Diversification across signals matters more than precise weights.
  3. Translation: map the composite score to a position, capped by limits (for example ±1 year of active duration).
  4. Risk scaling: shrink positions when volatility is high so each view uses a steady share of the risk budget.
  5. Overlay judgement: committees adjust for events the models cannot see, and record why.
  6. Review: track hit rates and information ratios by signal, not just the combined outcome.

Portfolio construction with a factor risk model

Fixed income risk models describe each bond by its exposures to a small set of factors (key rates by currency, spread factors by sector and rating, currency, idiosyncratic issuer risk) and estimate the covariance of those factors. The optimiser then chooses holdings to maximise expected active return for a given tracking error, subject to constraints: duration and key rate bands, sector and issuer limits, minimum liquidity, turnover and transaction costs. Mean–variance optimisers overreact to small differences in expected returns; practical fixes include shrinking return forecasts, adding turnover penalties, and resampling.

Benchmark design

  • Market-cap weighting problem: bond indices weight issuers by how much debt they have outstanding, so the most indebted issuers get the largest weights.
  • Duration drift: index duration changes as governments change issuance. A client's risk appetite does not.
  • Alternatives: capped issuer weights, GDP-weighted sovereign indices, duration-targeted or maturity-bucketed indices, and liability benchmarks.
  • Currency: for Australian investors, global bonds are usually benchmarked hedged to AUD, which removes currency volatility that bonds are not meant to carry.
  • Composite benchmarks for multi-sector portfolios should reflect the strategic allocation, and be rebalanced on a set schedule.

Scores for a duration decision: value +1.2 (real yields above neutral), carry +0.4 (curve modestly steep), momentum −0.8 (yields trending up), macro −0.2.

Risk parity and diversification in fixed income

Allocating capital equally across government bonds, IG, HY and EM leaves the portfolio dominated by the riskiest sleeves. Risk parity allocates so each sleeve contributes equally to total risk, which usually means more government duration and less high yield, sometimes with leverage. In fixed income multi-sector funds, a simpler version is to set allocations by target DTS and duration contributions rather than by capital weights.

Common process failures
  • Too many correlated signals that all measure the same thing, creating false confidence.
  • Overriding the process only when it is losing money.
  • Ignoring transaction costs in back-tests, especially in credit.
  • Sizing positions by conviction rather than by risk and track record.
  • Not recording the reasons for decisions, which makes review impossible.

Futures, swaps and currency hedging

Derivatives let you change duration, curve and currency exposure without trading the underlying bonds. They are how most active views are actually implemented.

Bond futures

Futures are the cheapest, most liquid way to add or remove duration. The number of contracts comes from closing the BPV gap.

Contracts = (BPV_target − BPV_portfolio) ÷ BPV_futures US Treasury futures: BPV_futures ≈ BPV_CTD ÷ conversion factor
  • US Treasury futures are physically delivered. The short chooses which bond to deliver, and the cheapest-to-deliver (CTD) drives the contract's risk. If the CTD switches, the hedge ratio changes.
  • ASX 3-year and 10-year Treasury bond futures are cash settled against a basket of ACGBs, priced as 100 minus yield, on a notional 6% coupon bond. Their BPV comes from pricing that notional bond at the futures yield.
  • Basis risk: the futures and your portfolio do not move one-for-one. Corporate bonds carry spread risk that a government futures hedge does not remove.

Interest rate swaps

Receiving fixed behaves like owning a bond funded at the floating rate: it adds duration. Paying fixed removes duration. Swaps are the standard LDI tool because they reach the long maturities liabilities need.

The swap spread (swap rate minus government yield) reflects bank credit, funding and balance sheet conditions, and the supply of government bonds. Heavy government issuance can push government yields above swaps (negative swap spreads), as has happened at the long end in the US.

Options and CDS

Swaptions and bond options add or sell convexity directly. Payer swaptions protect against rising rates; receivers against falling rates. CDS indices (such as CDX and iTraxx) are the liquid way to add or hedge credit beta quickly, while single-name CDS isolates one issuer.

Currency-hedged bonds

For an Australian investor buying offshore bonds, the currency decision is often bigger than the bond decision. Unhedged, you take full currency risk, which for bonds usually swamps the return you are trying to earn. Hedged, the forward contract locks in the rate but adds or subtracts the difference in short-term interest rates, by covered interest parity.

Hedged return ≈ foreign bond return + (domestic short rate − foreign short rate)

So a US Treasury yielding more than an ACGB is not automatically better. If US cash rates are above Australian cash rates, hedging costs the difference and can erase the pick-up. The cross-currency basis adds a further small adjustment in practice. Compare hedged yields, and compare curves on a hedged basis, when choosing where to own duration.

Worked example: ASX 10-year futures BPV

The ASX 10-year Treasury bond futures contract is priced as 100 minus yield on a notional $100,000, 6% semi-annual coupon, 10-year bond.

US Treasury futures: CTD and conversion factors

US bond futures are physically settled. The short can deliver any bond in a defined basket, and each bond's price is adjusted by a conversion factor (its price at a 6% yield ÷ 100). The bond that is cheapest to deliver drives the futures price, so:

Futures BPV ≈ BPV of the CTD ÷ conversion factor of the CTD

When yields are well above 6%, high-duration bonds tend to be CTD; well below 6%, low-duration bonds tend to be CTD. If the CTD switches, the futures' duration changes, and a hedge built on the old CTD becomes wrong. The basis (cash price − futures × conversion factor) is traded by basis desks.

Interest rate swaps: mechanics and uses
  • A receiver on $50m of a 10-year swap with a DV01 of about $800 per $1m adds about $40,000 per bp of duration.
  • Swaps settle on floating reference rates (BBSW or AONIA in Australia, SOFR in the US). Most are centrally cleared, with daily variation margin and initial margin.
  • Swap spread trades: buy government bonds and pay fixed on swaps (or the reverse) to express a view on the spread between them.
  • Asset swaps: buy a fixed-rate bond and pay fixed on a matching swap to keep the credit spread and remove rate risk.
Currency hedging, worked

An Australian investor buys a US Treasury. AUD/USD spot is 0.6600, the US 1-year rate 4.0% and the AUD rate 3.6%.

  1. By covered interest parity, the 1-year forward = 0.6600 × 1.040 ÷ 1.036 ≈ 0.6625.
  2. The investor sells USD forward to lock in the AUD value. Because AUD rates are lower, the forward price of AUD is higher than spot, so converting back costs about 0.4% over the year.
  3. A 4.2% Treasury yield becomes roughly 3.8% hedged. Compare that with a 4.4% ACGB.

Rolling hedges also create cash flows: if the AUD rises, the forward contracts gain and the bond loses in AUD terms (and vice versa), which requires liquidity to settle.

Collateral, counterparty and liquidity risk
  • Variation margin: derivative positions are marked daily and losses are paid in cash. A hedging overlay needs a liquidity buffer.
  • Initial margin for cleared and uncleared derivatives ties up assets.
  • Counterparty risk in uncleared trades is managed with credit support annexes (CSAs) and collateral.
  • Lesson from 2022: leveraged LDI overlays in the UK showed that a sharp move can create margin calls faster than assets can be sold. Stress test collateral needs, not just P&L.

The primary market: a corporate bookbuild

  1. Mandate: the issuer appoints banks as lead managers.
  2. Investor education: roadshow or calls, especially for new issuers.
  3. Initial price thoughts (IPTs): a spread range, set wide to attract orders.
  4. Book building: investors submit orders, often with spread limits. The leads revise guidance tighter as the book grows.
  5. Pricing: final spread and size are set, usually tighter than IPTs.
  6. Allocation: oversubscribed books are scaled back; long-term, price-setting investors are favoured.
  7. Free to trade: the bond begins secondary trading, often tightening if the new issue concession was generous.

Inflating orders to get a bigger allocation is common and risky: if the book weakens, you may be filled in full. Size orders to what you actually want.

The AOFM sells ACGBs mainly by competitive tender: bidders submit yields and amounts, bonds are allocated from the lowest yield up. The cost of a trade has two parts: the bid–offer spread you cross, and the market impact of your size pushing the price. Trading slowly reduces impact but exposes you to price moves while you wait (timing risk). The lab uses the widely used square-root impact model to show the trade-off.

Impact ≈ k × daily volatility × √(daily trade size ÷ daily volume)

  • Best execution: take reasonable steps to get the best overall result for the client, weighing price, cost, speed, likelihood of execution and size. Evidence it: record quotes received and why a counterparty was chosen.
  • Pre-trade compliance: check limits (issuer, rating, duration, liquidity, restricted lists) before trading.

Worked example: estimating the cost of a credit trade

You need to sell $40m of a corporate bond that trades about $10m a day. Bid–offer is 6 bp (so crossing costs about 3 bp), daily spread volatility is 4 bp, spread duration 6.

  1. Selling it all today: impact ≈ 0.8 × 4 bp × √(40 ÷ 10) = 6.4 bp, plus 3 bp spread cost: about 9.4 bp of spread, or roughly 0.56% of price with spread duration 6. On $40m that is about $225,000.
  2. Selling over four days: impact ≈ 0.8 × 4 × √(10 ÷ 10) = 3.2 bp, total cost about 6.2 bp. But you carry the unsold bonds through several days of spread moves.
  3. The right choice depends on your view and urgency. If you are selling because of bad news, speed matters more than impact.
How dealers make markets

Dealers quote prices and hold inventory, earning the bid–offer spread and managing risk with futures, swaps and CDS indices. Post-2008 capital and leverage rules made balance sheet more expensive, so dealers hold less inventory and act more as agents, matching buyers and sellers. That is why liquidity in credit can disappear in stress: dealers step back exactly when many investors want to sell. Building relationships with several dealers, and using all-to-all and portfolio trading, widens your sources of liquidity.

Trading futures and rolls

Bond futures expire quarterly. Before expiry, positions are rolled into the next contract by trading a calendar spread. The roll's price reflects carry and the delivery option, and can be rich or cheap. For ASX 3- and 10-year futures, check the roll price against fair value before rolling large positions.

Information barriers and MNPI

Pre-sounding a new issue, participating in a restructuring committee, or lending privately can give you material non-public information. You are then "wall-crossed" and restricted from trading the issuer's securities until the information is public. Compliance maintains restricted lists; the PM's job is to recognise when a conversation is about to cross the wall and to decide whether that is worth the restriction.

Risk budgeting

Allocate tracking error to the sources where you have the most skill. If duration timing is the least reliable source of alpha, it should use a small share of the budget even if it is the easiest position to take. Check that positions are sized so their contribution to risk matches their expected contribution to return: the information ratio of each idea, not just its conviction.

Performance attribution

Fixed income attribution uses risk factors, not the Brinson sector allocation approach used for equities. The Campisi framework is a common starting point:

Total return = Income effect (coupon / price) + Treasury effect (duration × change in government yields, split into parallel, slope, curvature) + Spread effect (spread duration × change in sector spread) + Selection effect (residual: issuer and security picks)

Run it for both portfolio and benchmark; the differences give active return by source. A good attribution report tells you whether you are being paid for the risks you intended to take.

Desk note

When a PM asks "why did we underperform last month?", start from the risk report, not the return report. Active duration × change in yields gets you most of the way, key rates explain the curve piece, and spread duration × change in spreads explains credit. What is left is selection. Being able to produce that walk in five minutes is a strong signal to a new team.

Worked example: ex-ante tracking error

A portfolio is 0.5 years long duration versus its benchmark. Annual yield volatility is 90 bp, so the rates contribution is 0.5 × 0.90% = 0.45%. Its active credit position has a standalone tracking error of 0.30%. The correlation between the two risk sources is −0.3.

TE = √(0.45² + 0.30² + 2 × (−0.3) × 0.45 × 0.30) = √(0.2025 + 0.09 − 0.081) = √0.2115 ≈ 0.46%

Negative correlation between rates and credit (spreads widen when government yields fall) means combined risk is less than the sum. In a stagflation-style shock where both lose together, that diversification disappears; stress tests capture this, tracking error models do not.

Worked example: parametric VaR

A portfolio has DV01 of $50,000. Daily yield volatility is 6 bp.

  1. Daily P&L standard deviation = $50,000 × 6 = $300,000.
  2. 95% one-day VaR = 1.645 × $300,000 ≈ $494,000.
  3. 99% = 2.33 × $300,000 ≈ $699,000.

VaR assumes normal returns and stable correlations. Real bond returns have fat tails, so pair VaR with expected shortfall and historical stress scenarios.

Global rules that drive demand

  • Basel III bank liquidity rules (the Liquidity Coverage Ratio and Net Stable Funding Ratio) require banks to hold high-quality liquid assets, making banks large buyers of government, semi and SSA bonds.
  • Solvency II and other insurance regimes set capital charges by rating and duration, which drive insurer demand for long IG credit.
  • UCITS (Europe) and the US Investment Company Act, including its liquidity risk management rule, set diversification, leverage and liquidity limits for funds.
  • US money market fund reforms shape demand for bills and commercial paper.
  • Central clearing and margin rules for derivatives affect the cost of swaps and the liquidity a portfolio must hold.

Valuation

Accounting standards (AASB 13 in Australia, IFRS 13 internationally) classify fair value inputs into three levels:

  • Level 1: quoted prices in active markets for identical assets (futures, on-the-run government bonds).
  • Level 2: observable inputs other than quoted prices (most corporate bonds, priced from dealer quotes or matrix pricing).
  • Level 3: significant unobservable inputs (private credit, illiquid structured products).

Good valuation governance means independent price verification, a documented pricing hierarchy, challenge of stale prices, and extra oversight of Level 3 assets, where the manager has the most discretion.

Performance reporting

The Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS), maintained by the CFA Institute, set rules for calculating and presenting performance fairly: all fee-paying discretionary portfolios in composites, time-weighted returns, consistent treatment of fees, and disclosure. For fixed income, also report risk alongside return: duration, tracking error and credit quality relative to the benchmark.

Go deeper: detail

How regulation creates relative value
  • Bonds that qualify as high-quality liquid assets for banks trade richer than similar bonds that do not.
  • Insurer demand for long-dated IG compresses long-end credit curves.
  • Rating-constrained investors must sell fallen angels, which creates forced-selling discounts.
  • Index inclusion rules (minimum size, rating, maturity) create predictable buying and selling at month-end.
  • Capital rules for bank AT1 and Tier 2 determine issuance and call decisions.
Time-weighted versus money-weighted returns

Time-weighted return removes the effect of client cash flows, measuring the manager's decisions; it is the standard for comparing managers. Money-weighted return (an internal rate of return) reflects the timing of flows and is standard for private credit and other closed-end funds, where the manager controls when capital is drawn and returned. Comparing a private credit IRR with a public bond fund's time-weighted return is not like for like.

Investment governance in practice
  • An investment policy statement sets objectives, benchmarks, risk limits and permitted instruments.
  • Investment committees approve strategy and review performance; minutes record decisions and dissent.
  • Limit breaches are escalated and remediated under a documented process.
  • New instruments (a first CLO or private credit fund, for example) go through a product approval process covering valuation, liquidity, operations and legal terms.

Bootstrap spot rates from annual par yields

def bootstrap(par):
    """par: annual-coupon par yields for 1..N years (decimals)."""
    dfs = []
    for n, c in enumerate(par, 1):
        dfs.append((1 - c * sum(dfs)) / (1 + c))
    return [df ** (-1 / n) - 1 for n, df in enumerate(dfs, 1)]

bootstrap([0.030, 0.035, 0.039])   # [0.0300, 0.0351, 0.0392]

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

def ns(m, b0, b1, b2, lam):
    x = m / lam
    g = (1 - np.exp(-x)) / x
    return b0 + b1 * g + b2 * (g - np.exp(-x))

mats = np.array([0.25, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 20, 30])

Key rate durations by bumping the curve

import numpy as np

def pv(cfs, times, zero):              # zero(t): continuously compounded
    return sum(cf * np.exp(-zero(t) * t) for cf, t in zip(cfs, times))

def weight(t, i, keys):                # triangular bump, sums to 1
    if t <= keys[0]:  return 1.0 if i == 0 else 0.0
    if t >= keys[-1]: return 1.0 if i == len(keys) - 1 else 0.0
    k = keys[i]
    if i > 0 and keys[i-1] < t <= k:
        return (t - keys[i-1]) / (k - keys[i-1])
    if i < len(keys) - 1 and k <= t < keys[i+1]:
        return (keys[i+1] - t) / (keys[i+1] - k)
    return 0.0

def krd(cfs, times, zero, keys=(2, 5, 10, 30), h=1e-4):
    p0, out = pv(cfs, times, zero), {}
    for i, k in enumerate(keys):
        up = lambda t: zero(t) + h * weight(t, i, keys)
        dn = lambda t: zero(t) - h * weight(t, i, keys)
        out[k] = (pv(cfs, times, dn) - pv(cfs, times, up)) / (2 * p0 * h)
    return out                          # sums to effective duration

Carry, roll and breakeven

def carry_roll(zero, m, h=1.0, funding=0.0):
    carry = (zero(m) - funding) * h
    roll = (m - h) * (zero(m) - zero(m - h))
    breakeven = (carry + roll) / (m - h)  # yield rise that wipes it out
    return carry, roll, breakeven

Module 28 · History

Case studies: when fixed income broke

Every major bond market episode exposed a risk that models underweighted. Knowing these cases gives you a stress-test library and the vocabulary senior PMs use.

By the end of this module you can
  • Explain what happened in each episode, and the mechanism that turned a shock into a crisis.
  • Draw the portfolio lesson from each, and turn it into a stress test or monitoring rule.
1994: the bond market massacre

What happened. The Fed began raising rates in February 1994 after years of low rates, and kept going, doubling the funds rate to 6% within a year. Markets had positioned for stable rates with leverage. US 10-year yields rose from about 5.2% to near 8% by late in the year. Orange County, California, which had borrowed heavily through repo to buy structured notes, lost about $1.6 billion and filed for bankruptcy in December.

Mechanism. Leveraged carry trades, mortgage convexity hedging (MBS extension forced hedgers to sell duration), and crowded positioning amplified the move.

Lesson. Carry is compensation for risk, not free money. Leverage plus negative convexity turns a policy surprise into a rout. Stress test: a fast 250 bp sell-off with extension in MBS.

1998: Russia and LTCM

What happened. Russia devalued and defaulted on domestic debt in August 1998. Investors fled to the safest, most liquid assets. Long-Term Capital Management, a highly leveraged hedge fund running convergence trades (long cheap off-the-run bonds, short rich on-the-run bonds, long swap spreads and credit), lost most of its capital. In September the New York Fed brokered a recapitalisation by a group of banks to prevent a disorderly unwind.

Mechanism. Relative value spreads that "had to converge" widened instead, because everyone wanted the same liquid assets at once. Many firms held similar positions, so selling begat selling.

Lesson. Liquidity premia widen in crises, and correlations go to one. Crowding matters: know who else owns your trade. Stress test: on-the-run/off-the-run, swap spreads and credit all widening together.

2008: the global financial crisis

What happened. Losses on US subprime mortgages spread through securitisations and CDOs held across the banking system. Funding markets froze; bill–OIS spreads blew out. Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008; the next day the Reserve Primary Fund broke the buck. US high yield spreads peaked near 2,000 bp and investment grade spreads above 600 bp. Governments and central banks responded with guarantees, capital injections, and QE.

Mechanism. Correlated mortgage defaults destroyed tranches rated AAA on the assumption of diversification. Short-term funding of long-term assets (in banks, conduits and money funds) turned losses into runs.

Lesson. Ratings are not risk measures; structure and correlation are. Funding liquidity and market liquidity collapse together. Stress test: extreme spread widening with frozen funding and forced selling.

2013: the taper tantrum

What happened. In May 2013 Fed Chair Bernanke signalled that asset purchases could slow. US 10-year yields rose from about 1.6% to nearly 3% within four months. Emerging market bonds and currencies sold off sharply, especially in countries with current account deficits.

Mechanism. Term premium compressed by QE snapped back. Investors who had reached for yield in EM and long duration exited together.

Lesson. Communication about the balance sheet can move term premium as much as rate changes. Stress test: a term-premium-led bear steepening with EM outflows.

March 2020: the dash for cash

What happened. As COVID spread, investors sold everything, including US Treasuries, to raise cash. Treasury market liquidity deteriorated badly, credit spreads gapped wider, and funds faced redemptions. The Fed cut to zero, launched unlimited QE and, for the first time, backstopped corporate bond markets. The RBA cut the cash rate to 0.25% and introduced a target for the 3-year yield.

Mechanism. Leveraged basis trades unwound, dealers' balance sheets were constrained, and open-ended funds sold liquid assets to meet redemptions.

Lesson. Even the most liquid market can dysfunction. Hold genuine liquidity buffers; do not rely on selling government bonds at screen prices in a panic. Stress test: simultaneous liquidity shock in rates and credit with redemptions.

2021: the end of the RBA's yield target

What happened. From late 2020, the RBA targeted a yield of 0.10% on the April 2024 ACGB. As inflation rose in 2021, markets priced earlier rate hikes. In late October the bond's yield surged far above the target and the RBA did not defend it; it formally discontinued the target in early November 2021. The RBA's own review later acknowledged the exit was disorderly and caused some reputational damage.

Mechanism. A yield target tied to a single bond near a changing policy outlook becomes a one-way bet for the market once the central bank's commitment is in doubt.

Lesson. Policy commitments are conditional. When the data turn, the market tests them. Stress test: a sharp front-end sell-off in ACGBs around a policy regime change.

2022: stocks and bonds fall together

What happened. Inflation surged and central banks hiked aggressively. Global bond indices had their worst year in decades; the US Aggregate index lost about 13%, its worst calendar year on record. Equities fell at the same time, so balanced portfolios lost their usual diversification.

Mechanism. An inflation shock hurts both bonds (higher yields) and equities (higher discount rates, margin pressure), flipping the stock–bond correlation positive.

Lesson. Bonds hedge growth shocks, not inflation shocks. Monitor the stock–bond correlation regime, and consider linkers, cash and other diversifiers when inflation risk dominates.

September 2022: the UK LDI crisis

What happened. The UK government's "mini-budget" on 23 September announced unfunded tax cuts. Long gilt yields rose over 100 bp in days. Pension funds using leveraged LDI (swaps and repo to hedge long liabilities) faced huge collateral calls and sold gilts to meet them, pushing yields up further. The Bank of England stepped in with temporary purchases of long-dated gilts on 28 September.

Mechanism. A doom loop: higher yields caused margin calls, which caused forced gilt sales, which caused higher yields. Collateral buffers were sized for normal moves, not for a move of this speed.

Lesson. Derivatives overlays turn market risk into liquidity risk. Size collateral buffers for extreme moves and know where the cash comes from.

March 2023: SVB and Credit Suisse

What happened. Silicon Valley Bank had invested deposits in long-dated Treasuries and agency MBS, much of it in held-to-maturity accounts, and was sitting on large unrealised losses after rates rose. When it announced a loss on securities sales and a capital raise, depositors, many uninsured and closely networked, withdrew about $42 billion in a day. It was closed on 10 March. Days later, Credit Suisse was taken over by UBS; the Swiss regulator ordered its AT1 bonds (about CHF 16 billion) written down to zero while shareholders received some value.

Mechanism. Duration mismatch plus a concentrated, flighty deposit base. Accounting classification hid the economic loss until liquidity forced its recognition. For Credit Suisse, the AT1 contract terms and regulator's powers overrode the usual hierarchy.

Lesson. Duration risk is real even if you do not mark to market. For bank capital securities, read the contract and understand the resolution regime; the textbook ranking may not apply.

Interview questions

1. Walk me through how you would decide the portfolio's duration position.

Start from what is priced: the forwards and OIS path versus my view of policy, informed by the central bank's reaction function and the data. Then valuation (real yields and term premium versus neutral), carry and roll, momentum, and positioning. Combine these into a view, size it to the risk budget in DV01 or active duration terms, express it in the most efficient instrument (usually futures), set a stop and a target, and identify catalysts. Finally, check how it interacts with the rest of the portfolio: is credit already adding duration-like risk in a sell-off?

2. The curve is inverted. Should we be long duration?

Not automatically. On an inverted curve, long duration has negative carry: you lose if yields do not fall. The trade works only if yields fall below the forwards, which requires cuts beyond what is priced. I would compare the priced cutting path with my own, look at term premium and valuation, and consider expressing a cut view in the front end (where the policy effect is strongest) or with a steepener, which usually benefits once cuts begin.

3. What is the difference between modified and effective duration, and why does it matter?

Modified duration assumes fixed cash flows and measures sensitivity to the bond's own yield. Effective duration reprices the bond when the benchmark curve shifts, letting cash flows change, so it captures embedded options. For a callable or an MBS, modified duration overstates rate sensitivity when the option is in the money. Risk systems use effective duration; using modified for optionable bonds gives the wrong hedge.

4. Explain term premium and how you would use it.

It is the extra yield for holding a long bond instead of rolling short bills, after allowing for the expected policy path. It is estimated with models like ACM or Kim–Wright, so I would focus on changes and regimes. High term premium means duration is better paid; low term premium means bonds are expensive insurance. I also watch the stock–bond correlation, because it drives whether investors are willing to accept a low premium for bonds' hedging value.

5. How do you decide whether credit spreads are attractive?

Compare spread with expected losses to get the risk premium, then compare that with history on a like-for-like basis (adjusting for rating mix and duration of the index). Look at the breakeven spread widening, the cycle (leverage trends, defaults, lending standards), technicals (supply, fund flows, all-in yield demand) and relative value against other risk assets. Tight spreads with high all-in yields often favour owning duration over spread.

6. What is a DV01-neutral steepener and what can go wrong?

Long the short end and short the long end, sized so each leg has equal DV01. It profits if the curve steepens and is insensitive to small parallel moves. Risks: the curve does not move one-for-one (historically the front end moves more in rallies), negative carry if the curve is steep, and curve moves already priced in the forwards. Check the carry, the PCA exposure to level, and whether the steepening is already priced.

7. A client asks why their bond fund lost money this year. What do you say?

Yields rose, so bond prices fell; the fund's duration determines how much. But higher yields mean the portfolio now earns more income, and over a horizon near its duration, higher yields tend to raise total returns. Show the income now being earned, the break-even, and how the fund did versus its benchmark and why (duration, curve, credit, selection).

8. How would you hedge a corporate bond portfolio against rising rates without selling bonds?

Sell government bond futures or pay fixed on swaps, sized so the BPV of the overlay offsets the portfolio's rate BPV. This keeps the spread exposure. Check basis risk (corporate yields do not move one-for-one with government yields, especially in risk-off moves when spreads widen as government yields fall) and the cash needed for margin.

9. What is the fulcrum security, and why does it matter?

In a restructuring, it is the tranche where the enterprise value runs out: partly covered, and likely to be converted into equity. It matters because its value depends on the enterprise value and the restructuring terms, not on the coupon. Distressed investors buy it to gain control. Identifying it tells you which of an issuer's bonds carry the real risk.

10. Compare investing in Australian RMBS with bank senior bonds.

Both are floating rate off BBSW with low rate duration. Bank senior is a single-name exposure to the bank; RMBS is exposure to a diversified pool of mortgages, protected by subordination, excess spread and often LMI, with the originator's servicing quality mattering. RMBS pays more for complexity, lower liquidity and extension risk around calls. Senior RMBS has strong historical performance, but analysis of the pool, the originator and the structure is essential.

11. What risks worry you most in private credit?

Hidden losses from smoothed marks, rising PIK and amendments masking stress, looser underwriting as capital floods in, concentration in construction or sponsor-backed cyclicals, liquidity mismatch in evergreen funds, and fees that take a large share of the illiquidity premium. I would ask for loss history through a cycle, the valuation process and independent review, and a look-through at PIK and non-accruals.

12. Should we hedge the currency on global bonds?

For a defensive bond allocation, usually yes: currency volatility dwarfs bond return and defeats the purpose of the allocation. Compare hedged yields, which add the domestic-minus-foreign short-rate differential. A partial unhedged position can make sense if the AUD tends to fall in risk-off episodes, giving portfolio diversification, but that is a currency decision and should be sized as such.

13. How would you build a fixed income TAA process?

Define a small set of economically sensible signals (valuation, carry, momentum, macro) for each decision (duration, curve, credit, country), standardise them, combine with simple weights, map to positions within limits, scale by volatility, and review by signal. Document judgement overrides and their reasons. Keep turnover and costs realistic.

14. What did 2022 teach you about bonds in multi-asset portfolios?

That bonds hedge growth shocks but not inflation shocks. When inflation drives markets, stock–bond correlation turns positive, so the defensive sleeve needs other diversifiers: cash, linkers, shorter duration, and currency. It also showed that starting yields matter: bonds entered 2022 with very little income to cushion price falls.

15. Tell me about a trade idea you have right now.

Use the pitch template: thesis versus what is priced, expression, size in DV01, carry and roll, target and stop, catalysts, and what breaks it. Keep it to two minutes, with numbers. Interviewers care about process and risk thinking more than whether the view turns out right.

16. Break down the 10-year yield for me.

Split it into the expected path and the risk premium, then into real and inflation. Expected path: the average cash rate the market and models expect over ten years, anchored by OIS at the front and the neutral rate further out. Subtract that from the yield to get the term premium. Then use the linker breakeven: compare it with inflation expectations from surveys to separate expected inflation from the inflation risk premium, and the remainder is the real term premium. Mention convexity at the long end and technicals such as supply and the government–OIS spread. Finish with what it means: for example, "most of the yield is premium, so duration is reasonably paid even without a cutting cycle".

P&L drills

Drill 1: a rates sell-off

Setup. $500m portfolio, modified duration 6.2, convexity 60. Benchmark duration 6.0. Yields rise 40 bp in parallel over a month. Portfolio earns 0.35% income over the month.

Question. Estimate the portfolio's return and active return (ignore benchmark convexity differences).

Answer. Price effect: −6.2 × 0.40% + ½ × 60 × 0.004² = −2.48% + 0.05% = −2.43%. Plus income 0.35%: total about −2.08%, or −$10.4m. Benchmark price effect about −6.0 × 0.40% = −2.40% (plus similar income). Active return from duration ≈ −0.2 × 0.40% = −0.08%, about −$400,000.

Drill 2: a curve move with a duration-neutral book

Setup. Portfolio and benchmark both have duration 6.0. Key rate durations: portfolio 2y 1.0, 10y 3.5; benchmark 2y 1.5, 10y 3.0 (other buckets equal). The 2-year yield falls 20 bp and the 10-year rises 10 bp.

Question. Active return?

Answer. Active KRDs: 2y −0.5, 10y +0.5. Return from 2y: −(−0.5) × (−0.20%) = −0.10%. From 10y: −(+0.5) × (+0.10%) = −0.05%. Total −0.15%. The portfolio was positioned for flattening and the curve steepened.

Drill 3: credit spreads widen

Setup. $200m IG credit portfolio, spread duration 5.5, average spread 110 bp. Over a month, spreads widen 25 bp; government yields fall 15 bp; rate duration 5.8.

Question. Estimate the price return, separating rates and spread effects.

Answer. Rates: −5.8 × (−0.15%) = +0.87%. Spread: −5.5 × 0.25% = −1.375%. Spread carry for a month: 1.10% ÷ 12 ≈ +0.09%. Net about −0.41% excluding government-curve income, about −$0.8m. Rates hedged a large part of the spread loss, as is typical in risk-off moves.

Drill 4: a futures overlay

Setup. $300m portfolio at duration 4.0. The committee wants 5.0. ASX 10-year futures BPV is $87 per contract.

Question. How many contracts, and what is the P&L on the overlay if yields then fall 20 bp?

Answer. BPV gap = $300m × 1.0 × 0.0001 = $30,000. Contracts = 30,000 ÷ 87 ≈ 345, bought. P&L if yields fall 20 bp: 345 × $87 × 20 ≈ +$600,000 (ignoring basis).

Drill 5: a hedged offshore bond

Setup. An AUD investor holds a hedged US Treasury position. Over a year the Treasury's local return is 3.0%. US cash averaged 4.2%, AUD cash 3.7%. The AUD rose 5% against the USD.

Question. What was the approximate AUD return?

Answer. Hedged return ≈ 3.0% + (3.7% − 4.2%) = 2.5%. The 5% currency move is almost fully offset by the hedge (small residual from hedging the starting value only). Unhedged, the investor would have lost about 2%.
